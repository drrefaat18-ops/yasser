| ID | severity | location | finding | why it matters | suggested fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-001 | major | Chapter 7, §§7.4 and 7.7; worked example “Pricing a new hormone test”; Q3 | The book confuses markup with gross margin. Multiplying cost by `1 + 25%` gives a 25% markup, not a 25% margin. For a true 25% margin, the 240 EGP cost requires `240 ÷ 0.75 = 320 EGP`. Likewise, Q3’s 200 EGP cost and “20% margin” require 250 EGP, but no option gives that answer; the keyed 240 EGP is a 20% markup. | This makes a worked pricing result and a numeric MCQ wrong as written. Students will learn the wrong formula for a common business measure. | Replace “margin” with “markup” throughout, or use `P = C ÷ (1 − margin)` and change the worked answers and MCQ options. |
| C-002 | major | Chapter 11, §11.4, “Five methods are used”; Table 11.1; glossary entry for COI | Cost-of-illness analysis is presented as one of five methods of economic evaluation even though §11.4 correctly says it compares no interventions. This contradicts Chapter 10’s definition that a true economic evaluation compares costs and consequences for two or more alternatives. COI is a descriptive/partial economic study, not a full economic evaluation. | The classification is a core learning outcome and can cause students to select COI incorrectly when asked to compare interventions. | Present CMA, CEA, CUA and CBA as comparative evaluation methods; place COI under “related descriptive economic studies” and revise the table, glossary and assessments accordingly. |
| C-003 | major | Chapter 3, §3.8 and references | The 2026–2027 edition cites only Law No. 151 of 2020 and tells readers to check future executive regulations. Executive Regulations No. 816 of 2025 are already in force and introduce operational requirements including licences or permits for controllers, processors and electronic marketing, with a compliance period ending in November 2026. The chapter’s four-rule checklist omits these material requirements. [Official PDPC framework](https://pdpc.gov.eg/) | A laboratory following the chapter could treat consent and security as sufficient while overlooking current licensing and governance obligations. | Cite Decree No. 816 of 2025 and update the checklist to cover applicability, electronic-marketing authorisation, controller/processor licensing, DPO requirements where applicable, and the current transition deadline. |
| C-004 | major | Chapter 4, §4.3 and Q3; Chapter 5, Table 5.1, §§5.1 and 5.1 “Through Two Lenses” | Service characteristics are stated as absolutes: no time between production and consumption and no storage. That is not true of the book’s main laboratory example: samples may be stored or queued, testing is performed after attendance, and reports are consumed later. Chapter 5 itself then says samples may wait and laboratory work may be batched. | This is a central LO1 definition, an internal contradiction, and the basis of an MCQ rationale. It confuses perishable service capacity with storable inputs and outputs. | Qualify these as tendencies. Separate the patient-facing collection encounter from analytical production and reporting, and define perishability as unused capacity that cannot be inventoried. Revise Q3’s absolute wording. |
| C-005 | major | Chapter 12, worked example “Cost per case detected for two screening tests,” Step 3 | The conclusion says Test B is worth adopting if early treatment avoids complications costing more than the 5,000 EGP ICER. That does not follow. The ICER is per additional case detected; the expected monetary benefit depends on the probability that an extra detected case avoids complications, the size of those avoided costs, confirmatory and treatment costs, and other health outcomes. | This applies an invalid decision rule and can reverse an actual screening decision. | Compare 5,000 EGP with an explicit willingness-to-pay threshold per additional case detected, or model downstream treatment costs and health outcomes to obtain incremental net benefit or cost per QALY. |
| C-006 | minor | Chapter 11, worked example “Discounting a future saving” | The printed intermediate value and result do not reproduce each other: `10,000 ÷ 1.159 = 8,628`, not 8,626 EGP. The 8,626 result comes from using the unrounded factor `1.03⁵ = 1.159274…`. | The book promises that every printed step can be followed exactly. | Print the factor as 1.159274, or retain 1.159 and report 8,628 EGP. |
| C-007 | minor | Chapter 2, §2.7 Egyptian Context: “contracted providers are chosen by the insurer” | This wording omits and obscures beneficiaries’ freedom to choose among contracted, accredited providers. UHIA chooses its network, but UHIA’s own description expressly states that beneficiaries retain provider choice within that network. [UHIA provider-network statement](https://uhia.gov.eg/495-%D8%AC%D9%87%D8%A9-%D8%AD%D8%A7%D8%B5%D9%84%D8%A9-%D8%B9%D9%84%D9%89-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A7%D8%B9%D8%AA%D9%85%D8%A7%D8%AF-%D9%85%D8%AA%D8%B9%D8%A7%D9%82%D8%AF%D8%A9-%D9%85%D8%B9-%D9%85%D9%86%D8%B8/) | Students may infer that the payer assigns the provider and that patients have no choice. | Say that UHIA contracts with GAHAR-accredited providers and beneficiaries choose among the available contracted providers, subject to referral pathways. |
| C-008 | minor | Chapter 12, Q3 and rationale | Q3 states only that two brands have “demonstrated equivalent effects,” then keys CMA. The chapter itself correctly requires equivalence across all relevant outcomes, including safety and adherence. “Equivalent effects” alone does not establish that condition, so CEA or “insufficient information” remains defensible. | The item contradicts the taught CMA decision rule and is underdetermined. | Change the stem to “demonstrated equivalent efficacy, safety, adherence and other relevant outcomes,” or add an “insufficient information” option and key it. |
| C-009 | minor | Chapter 12, “Key Formula: The ratios of this chapter” and §12.4 | The incremental B/C formula and below/above-one rule are presented without stating that alternatives must be ordered by increasing cost so that incremental cost is positive. With negative incremental cost, the ratio’s sign becomes misleading, just as with a negative ICER. | Students may apply the printed rule mechanically to an invalid ordering and make the wrong decision. | State the ordering condition, eliminate dominated alternatives first, and prefer incremental net benefit when signs are awkward. |
| C-010 | minor | Chapter 8, Figure/Table 8.2, zero-level example | The chapter defines channel levels by intermediaries between producer and patient, but labels “manufacturer supplying a hospital directly” as zero-level while the next row treats a hospital pharmacy between producer and patient as one-level. The identity of the final customer changes without explanation. | The same transaction can receive two different channel classifications under the printed table. | Distinguish institutional channels from patient-level channels, or use a true producer-to-final-user example for zero-level distribution. |
| C-011 | minor | Chapter 5, §5.4: “When perceived benefit exceeds total cost, customers are satisfied, return and recommend” | Positive customer value does not guarantee satisfaction, repeat use or recommendation. Chapter 1 correctly defines satisfaction by performance relative to expectations, so the categorical claim is internally inconsistent. | It conflates distinct marketing constructs and overstates what the value calculation establishes. | Change “are” to “are more likely to be,” and retain expectations as the direct basis of satisfaction. |
