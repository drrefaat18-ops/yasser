| C-001 | major | Chapter 12, §§12.3, Table 12.2 and “Three medicines for type 2 diabetes”; Q5 and E1 answers | Costs are for a 30-day supply, but effects are measured after three months. The printed CERs and −0.9 ICER therefore combine incompatible time horizons. If three 30-day supplies produced the three-month effects, medicine 1 versus 3 is (54−81)÷(25−15)=−2.7 EGP per percentage point; the CMA saving between medicines 1 and 2 is 51 EGP, not 17 EGP. Dominance is unchanged. | This is a wrong-unit health-economic result repeated in a worked example, MCQ key, essay answer and ledger correction E-005. | Put costs and effects on the same horizon. Either use three-month costs throughout or provide one-month effects. Update every repeated ratio and ledger status. |
| C-002 | major | Chapters 1–2, especially §2.4, Q6 and E3; repeated in Chapters 10 and 12 | Present-tense claims that a “large share” of Egyptian expenditure is out of pocket and that laboratory/imaging tests “very often” are self-paid cite only Law No. 2 of 2018. That statute does not supply expenditure data or a diagnostic-service payment share. Current WHO evidence supports the overall claim—54.9% of current health expenditure in 2022—but not the laboratory/imaging-specific extension. [WHO Egypt health-system review](https://applications.emro.who.int/docs/9789292746926-eng.pdf) | The unsupported extension is taught repeatedly and used as the premise of an MCQ key. This is a claim-support mismatch, even though the broader national claim is true. | Cite current WHO/National Health Accounts evidence for overall out-of-pocket expenditure. Remove or separately source the laboratory/imaging-specific claim. |
| C-003 | major | Chapter 9, §9.3 and E1 model answer: “careful pull toward the public for…check-up packages” | The model answer treats accurate claims and advice to consult a doctor as sufficient safeguards for public promotion of self-paid check-up packages. Chapter 1 correctly says such packages are unacceptable when tests are unnecessary. The later answer omits the controlling clinical-need rule and therefore permits demand generation for potentially unnecessary testing. | Students could infer that truthful wording makes package promotion ethical even when the included tests lack an evidence-based indication. | Replace the example with an evidence-based screening service, state eligibility criteria, and require clinical appropriateness before any public promotion. |
| C-004 | minor | Chapter 1, §1.2; consolidated references; ledger E-013 | The book calls the current AMA definition “approved in 2007” and cites Kotler and Keller (2016). The AMA identifies its current definition as approved in 2017, which a 2016 source cannot establish. [Official AMA definition](https://www.ama.org/the-definition-of-marketing-what-is-marketing/) | The central definition is worded correctly, but its date and provenance are wrong; ledger E-013 is not fully corrected. | Cite the AMA directly as the 2017-approved definition. Discuss the identical 2007 formulation separately if historically relevant. |
| C-005 | minor | Chapter 9, §9.8 and references: “Egyptian Drug Authority. (2021). Prescription medicine promotion guidelines” | The official EDA regulatory index dates the *Guideline for Prescription Medicine Promotion*, Version 01, to 2020, not 2021. The 2021 entry is a different guideline for online promotional, educational and awareness materials. [Official EDA index](https://edaegypt.gov.eg/en/the-regulatory-reference-of-the-egyptian-drug-authority-eda/regulatory-guidelines/ca-of-pharmaceutical-care/) | The cited document exists and supports the substantive pre-clearance and professional-audience claims, but its metadata are wrong in both reference lists. | Change the year to 2020 throughout. |
| C-006 | minor | Chapter 2, §2.5 and Q9 rationale: emergency care bought “whatever it costs” or at “almost any price” | This equates inelastic demand with perfectly inelastic demand. Inelastic means proportionately less responsive to price, not unlimited willingness or ability to pay. | It reinforces exactly the elasticity misconception the chapter is intended to correct and understates affordability barriers. | Say that urgent demand is generally less price-responsive, while affordability, substitutes and access still limit use. |
| C-007 | minor | Chapter 10, §§10.4 and 10.6 | Section 10.4 says willingness to pay can value intangible costs; §10.6 then says WTP belongs on the benefit side and is not added to costs. Both framings can be made equivalent, but the book presents them as incompatible classification rules without explaining the sign convention. | Students may double-count pain avoided or place the same monetised value on both sides of a CBA. | Adopt one convention—preferably WTP for avoiding harm as a monetary benefit—or explicitly explain the equivalent alternative and prohibit double-counting. |
| C-008 | minor | Chapter 11, §11.2 and Glossary definition of “utility” | Utility is described only by the anchors 1=full health and 0=death, without stating that some instruments permit negative values for states considered worse than death. | The definition is incomplete and can lead students to reject valid negative utilities and miscalculate QALYs. | Add that utilities can be below zero for health states valued as worse than death. |
| C-009 | minor | Chapter 4, Key Takeaways versus §4.3; Chapter 5, §5.1 | The takeaway says services “are produced and consumed together, and cannot be stored,” while the chapter body correctly qualifies that laboratory analysis and reporting may occur later and that service capacity—not samples or outputs—is perishable. | The summary reverses the careful qualification in the teaching text and invites an overgeneralised exam answer. | State that production and consumption often overlap and that unused service capacity is perishable. |
