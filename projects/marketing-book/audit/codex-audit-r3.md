| ID | severity | location | finding | why it matters | suggested fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-001 | major | Glossary, “cost-utility ratio — Cost per QALY gained; the lower, the better.” | This contradicts Chapters 11–12, which correctly warn that average cost per QALY must not rank alternatives. “QALY gained” normally denotes an incremental effect, whereas the glossary does not specify an incremental cost or comparator. | Students could select treatments using average ratios—the exact decision error the main text teaches them to avoid. | Define the incremental cost-utility ratio as Δcost ÷ ΔQALYs and state that decisions require incremental comparison, dominance checks and the relevant threshold. If retaining an average ratio, label it descriptive only. |
| C-002 | major | Chapter 11 §11.2, “production gained when a patient returns to work, an indirect monetary benefit,” versus Chapter 10 §§10.4–10.5 | Chapter 10 records lost productivity as an indirect cost, while Chapter 11 permits regained production to be recorded as a benefit without warning against using both conventions together. The same productivity change could therefore be counted once as an avoided cost and again as a benefit. | Double-counting can materially reverse CBA or societal-perspective conclusions. | Choose one accounting convention for productivity—cost offset or benefit—and explicitly prohibit counting the same change on both sides. Apply the same rule to treatment-cost savings. |
| C-003 | major | Chapter 11 §11.4, COI: “It estimates the most that could be saved if the disease were eliminated.” | A cost-of-illness total is not an estimate or valid upper bound of realizable savings. Elimination has intervention costs; fixed/shared costs may remain; avoided mortality can increase later-life costs; and not all attributed burden is preventable. COI provides no information about prevention costs ([BMJ review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1127320/)). | It encourages the classic error of treating disease burden as the economic benefit of prevention or elimination. | Replace with: “COI estimates the current economic burden; it does not estimate avoidable savings or establish that prevention is cost-effective.” |
| C-004 | major | Chapter 12 §12.4, CBA worked example and “each option’s own ratio says whether it is worth doing at all” | The conclusion that neither treatment is worth doing assumes an unprinted do-nothing comparator with zero cost and zero benefit. Standalone totals cannot establish acceptability unless their costs and benefits are explicitly incremental against the relevant baseline. | This is a wrong decision rule in the chapter whose objective is applying B/C rules. It can lead students to reject an option without comparing it with current care. | Add the status-quo/do-nothing alternative and state that every cost and benefit is incremental against it, or remove the “worth doing at all” conclusion and limit interpretation to B versus A. |
| C-005 | minor | Chapter 11 MCQ Q5 | “Using a common measure of health” does not uniquely require CUA. Conventional CEA can compare programmes in different diseases when they share a natural outcome such as life-years gained. The keyed answer becomes unique only if the common measure is specified as QALYs. | The item has a defensible alternative and tests inference from missing information. | Change the stem to “using QALYs as the common measure” or ask which method enables comparisons using quality-adjusted survival. |
| C-006 | minor | Chapter 4 §4.3, “A patient who sees opened needles packed in sealed wrappers…” | An opened needle cannot still be protected by an intact sealed wrapper. The sentence reverses the intended infection-control signal. | This is unsafe wording in a healthcare textbook, even though the intended meaning is evident. | Change “opened needles” to “unopened, sterile needles in intact sealed wrappers.” |
| C-007 | minor | Chapter 1 §1.2, current AMA definition “as cited in Kotler & Keller, 2016” | A 2016 secondary source cannot support the claim that the definition was approved/reapproved in 2017. The wording existed earlier, but the book specifically presents its current 2017 status without citing the AMA itself. The consolidated references omit the AMA source. | This is a claim–support mismatch in the book’s foundational definition. | Cite the [AMA definition page](https://www.ama.org/the-definition-of-marketing-what-is-marketing/) directly for the 2017 approval/current status; retain Kotler and Keller only for their own discussion. |
| C-008 | minor | Chapter 11 §11.4, “Two antibiotics have the same cure rate, 90%… Because outcomes are equivalent, CMA applies.” | Equal reported point estimates are not evidence of equivalence. CMA requires an appropriately designed equivalence/non-inferiority analysis within a prespecified margin and equivalence across all relevant outcomes. | The example weakens the correct rule stated immediately above it and may teach students to infer equivalence from matching percentages. | State that trials demonstrated equivalence within a specified margin for efficacy, safety, adherence and other relevant outcomes; otherwise use CEA. |
