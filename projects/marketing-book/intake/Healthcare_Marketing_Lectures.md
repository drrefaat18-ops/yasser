## The Marketing Concept

Marketing is **the process of planning and executing the conception, pricing, promotion, and distribution of goods, services, and ideas to create exchanges that satisfy individual and organizational objectives.**

### Digital Marketing

Also known as **online marketing**: advertising delivered through digital channels to promote brands and connect with potential customers using the internet and other forms of digital communication, such as:

- Search engines
- Websites
- Social media
- Email
- Mobile apps

---

## Components of the Marketing Process

1. Needs, wants, demands
2. Products and services
3. Value, satisfaction and quality
4. Exchange, transactions and relationships
5. Markets

---

## The Scope of Marketing

### 1. Create Awareness

- Informing customers about the company's products is a must for attracting them to buy.
- Marketing is the medium through which companies communicate with the public and explain the features or benefits of their products.

### 2. Study What Customers Want

- Marketing helps in understanding the needs or wants of customers, which enables companies to provide satisfactory services.
- Through their marketing programs, businesses interact with customers and understand their behaviour.
- A proper understanding of customer demand helps in designing the right product to satisfy their needs.

### 3. Product Planning

- A product is **a bundle of benefits that offers satisfaction to consumers**.
- Product planning starts with the generation of the idea and continues until the product is ready to be launched.
- To create a successful product, the company must understand consumer needs and the competition currently available in the market.

### 4. After-Sales Services

- The processes that make sure customers are satisfied with the organization's products and services.
- They ensure products and services meet or exceed customer expectations.
- **Examples of after-sales service:**
  - Warranty information
  - Product or service training
  - Repair or upgrade offers
  - Request for review
  - Return and exchange policy details
  - Automated support resources
  - Coupon or digital code
  - Survey

### 5. Pricing Policies

- **Price** is the amount of money charged for a product or service, or the sum of the values consumers exchange for the benefits of having or using it.
- Pricing involves the marketer determining the optimum price for a product.
- Good pricing policies help attract more consumers.
- Consumers are generally **price elastic**: the lower the price, the higher the demand; the higher the price, the lower the demand.
- Factors that influence price: manufacturing cost, government policies, marketing, competitors' prices, etc.

### 6. Distribution

- Selecting the proper distribution channel is necessary to attract new consumers.
- Choosing a channel means defining the route the goods take from the producer to the ultimate consumer.
- **Wholesaling** and **retailing** are the two most popular distribution channels.

### 7. Selling

- Selling is the supply of goods and services, directly or indirectly, to target consumers.
- It involves managing several activities at once: approaching new consumers, distributing free samples, making sales at big discounts, and getting feedback.

### 8. Packaging

- Packaging delivers the product safely and builds a good image in the consumer's mind.
- It involves designing and producing the external covering that keeps the product safe and hygienic.
- Packaging carries product information, which adds to the product's appeal and helps sales promotion.

### 9. Advertising

- Advertising is the best tool for marketing; it makes consumers aware of the product about to hit the market.
- It can increase sales drastically, and ultimately profits.
- Media: newspapers, television, radio, magazines, hoardings (billboards), the internet, etc.

### 10. Collecting Feedback

- Collecting customers' feedback or suggestions once the product is sold.
- This identifies customer satisfaction or dissatisfaction and helps improve service quality using their suggestions.

---

**Marketing mix:** "the set of actions, or tactics, that a company uses to promote its brand or product in the market."

**Marketing mix principles** — also known as the 4 Ps: "the key factors involved in the marketing of a good or service": **Product, Price, Place, Promotion.**

### 1. Product

- Products also include a range of factors such as packaging, quality, warranties, after-sales service and branding.

- Presence of instructions on medical application

- Qualification of pharmaceutical personnel

## Classifications of Consumer Goods

1. **Daily demand** — goods that satisfy basic daily needs and emergencies. *E.g., food, water, clothes.*
2. **Previous choice** — goods that the consumer, while choosing and buying, compares on suitability, quality, price and appearance. *E.g., watches, handbags, mobiles.*
3. **Special demand** — products with unique characteristics, for which a considerable share of buyers is ready to make extra effort. *E.g., drugs for a patient, air conditioners in hot countries, cars for transport.*
4. **Passive demand** — products the buyer does not know about, or knows about but mostly does not think of buying. *E.g., energy-efficiency measures, engines.*

### By Durability / Material Tangibility

- **Long-use (durable) goods:** not consumed or destroyed by use; last a long time — usable for at least three years. *E.g., office furniture, electronics, motorcycles, jewellery.*
- **Short-term (non-durable) goods:** short lifespan; usually consumed in one use or after a short period. *E.g., food, cosmetics, laundry detergent.*

### Durable vs. Non-Durable Goods

| Durable goods | Non-durable goods |
|---|---|
| Hard goods | Soft goods |
| Used for a long period of time | Used for a short period of time only |
| Examples: cars, books, television, freezer, etc. | Examples: petrol, cosmetics, frozen items, soaps, etc. |
| Can be used many times | Can be used only a limited number of times, in some cases only once |
| Can be resold after some years | Resale opportunity does not exist |

## Pharmaceutical Goods

Pharmaceutical goods are a special group divided into two subgroups: **medical products** and **products of medical appointment (PMA)**.

### Pharmaceuticals (Medicines)

Any substance or combination of substances (in a certain medicinal form) intended for the treatment or prevention of human disease, for diagnosis, or for the restoration, correction or change of physiological functions — packed and placed on the market.

### Classification of Medical Products

- **Anatomical principle** — preparations acting on the respiratory, cardiovascular, digestive system, etc.
- **Therapeutic application** — analgesics, antibiotics, hypotensive agents, etc.
- **Pharmacological action** — diuretics, anticoagulants, neuroleptics, etc.
- **Chemical structure** — alkaloids, cardiac glycosides, hormonal agents, etc.
- **Kind of control and circulation order** — narcotics, psychotropic substances.
- **Prescription and non-prescription drugs.**
- **Direction of use** — internal, external.
- **Physical and chemical properties and environmental sensitivity** — photosensitive, thermolabile, dyes, etc.
- **Kind of dosage form** — solutions, suspensions, tablets, ointments, etc.
- **Ready-made drugs (industrial production) vs. individually compounded medicines.**

### Products of Medical Appointment (PMA)

Medical-technical products, materials and medical products intended for the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of disease in the human body and/or support of these processes.

**Criteria for classifying PMA:**

- **By material** — rubber, glass, plastic products, etc.
- **By assortment group** — dressings, perfumery-cosmetic products, sanitary and hygiene items, etc.
- **By functional purpose** — patient-care items, medical tools, devices and equipment.
- **By storage group** — rubber products, dressings and auxiliary materials, plastic products, medical-technique products.

## Consumer Value

**Consumer value** is how much customers perceive your products or services to be worth. Customers must believe the product holds greater value than its price.

> **Consumer value (CV) = Perceived customer benefit − Total customer cost**

If perceived benefit > total cost → better customer satisfaction, more purchases, more profit.

### Types of Consumer Value

- **Functional value:** what an offer does — the solution it provides to the customer.
- **Monetary value:** the price paid relative to the offering's perceived worth; invites a trade-off between other values and monetary cost.
- **Social value:** how far owning a product or using a service lets the consumer connect with others.
- **Psychological value:** how far a product lets consumers express themselves or feel better.

### Properties of Goods That Achieve Consumer Value

- Quality — reliability, suitability, design
- Price matching quality
- Accompanying documentation
- Quality after-sales service
- A variety of assortment (opportunity of choice)

### Properties of Medical Products That Achieve Consumer Value

- Therapeutic efficiency
- Safety
- Convenience of application
- Profitability

## The Product Life Cycle (PLC)

- Products tend to go through different stages.
- The length of a product's life cycle is not fixed; it can last from weeks to years depending on the product.
- Four stages: **Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline** (sales volume plotted against time).

### 1. Introduction

**Definition:** the period during which initial market acceptance may be in doubt — often a period of slow growth.

**Properties:**

- Profits are often non-existent because of high marketing and other expenses.
- Marketing strategy is based on different combinations of product, price, promotion and distribution.
- Production costs, marketing costs and prices are traditionally high.
- Unless the product can be made efficiently with existing equipment despite unpredictable sales, mass-production economies are hard to achieve.

**In the pharmaceutical market:**

- The market consists of a small percentage of the medical population who are normally first to try new drugs and are influential with colleagues.
- If the product is as effective and safe as this core group wishes, word filters down to the rest of the medical community.
- Direct competition may not be a problem, but if the product merely does a job in a new or better way, it may be hard to convince physicians of the value of the change.

### 2. Growth

**Strategy:**

- (a) Product improvement — new features and models
- (b) Development of new market segments
- (c) Selective demand stimulation
- (d) Price adjustments

**Properties:**

- Substantial improvement in profits.
- Widespread approval of the product concept.
- The number of competitors begins to increase.
- Modifications of the original product, or completely different products for the same purpose, appear.
- Production methods are established, often with lower costs.
- Prices tend to fall for two reasons:
  1. Increased sales make economies of scale possible.
  2. More competing firms push prices down, in theory and in practice.

### 3. Maturity

Sales growth slows and profits peak, then begin to decline.

**Strategy:**

- (a) Search for new markets and new, varied uses for the product
- (b) Improve product quality through changes
- (c) New marketing-mix perspectives

**In the pharmaceutical market:**

- All product variations appear: tablets, capsules, liquid, a dermatologic form, a long-acting form, or a combination.
- Promotion tries to add vitality by segmenting the market with special messages for different physician specialties.

**Properties:**

- Competition reaches its peak.
- All firms hoping for market share are pursuing it; some are marginal financially or technologically.
- Total sales of the product class keep rising, but at a decreasing rate — the net effect is price competition.

### 4. Decline

**Properties:**

- A declining product is not necessarily unprofitable.
- Reasons for decline include:
  - The product's effectiveness compared with other therapies.
  - If caused by a new product, decline may be rapid — only those slow to change continue to prescribe it.
- Promotion may again aim at stimulating primary demand.
- The effort may be half-hearted, aimed only at a core of physician-users.
- Some firms drop out, leaving an opportunity for those remaining.

## The Price

- The price is **an exchange equivalent of the cost of goods or services in money terms**.
- Price represents **the value of a drug product for both the buyer and the seller**.

---

## Functions of Price

1. **Registration (accounting):** price is the means of accounting for cost indexes — volume of commodity circulation, volume of commodity stocks, arrival of goods, profitability.
2. **Stimulating:** price has a stimulating influence on manufacturers and consumers.
3. **Distributive:** distributes national income between branches of the economy, regions of the country and different social groups.
4. **Balancing supply and demand:** through prices, the link between production and consumption, supply and demand, is carried out.

---

## Classification of Prices

### 1. By Degree of State Influence

**1.1 Free prices**

- Formed according to market conditions, without state intervention.
- Based on free agreement between seller and buyer (supply & demand).
- State bodies do not take part in forming them but carry out control functions.
- *Examples: the free market; paying a worker's salary in private companies.*

**1.2 Controlled prices**

- The state sets an upper limit that prices may not exceed.
- Applies to vital goods and services: medical products, strategic raw materials, energy carriers, public transport, first-necessity consumer products.

**1.3 Fixed prices**

- State bodies fix these prices in planning or other documents; neither manufacturers nor sellers may change them.
- *Examples: study fees, gas or electricity bills, property taxes.*

### 2. By Form and Sphere of Trade

- **2.1 Wholesale prices** (enterprise wholesale prices, industry wholesale prices): prices at which enterprises' production is sold to and bought by firms and organizations.
- **2.2 Retail prices:** prices at which goods are sold to the population.
- **2.3 Tariffs:** prices (quotations) that define the level of payment for services.

### 3. By Type of Free Market

- **3.1 Auction price:** the direct price of goods sold at auction. It can differ substantially from the market price (usually higher), as it reflects unique or rare properties of the goods and depends on the auctioneer's skill.
- **3.2 Stock quote:** the price of goods, securities and services traded on an exchange, formed by supply and demand (stock market).

### 4. World Market Prices

Prices actually established for a given group of goods on the world market and recognized by international trade organizations for a certain period. *Example: gold and dollar prices.*

---

## Pricing Methods

| Category | Methods |
|---|---|
| Cost-based | Cost-plus, markup, break-even, target pricing & marginal cost |
| Demand-based | Price skimming, price penetration |
| Competition-based | Discount, premium, going rate |
| Other types | Target, value-based, psychological, bundled |

### Cost-Based Pricing

A method based on the cost of production, manufacturing and distribution. The price is set by adding a percentage to the costs to make a profit. Two types:

1. **Cost-plus pricing** — pricing your product at a certain margin above your costs.
2. **Break-even pricing** — the price at which total sales equal total costs (the break-even point); below it is loss, above it is profit.

### Demand-Based Pricing

Consumer demand determines the price. It aims to optimize revenue and profit by charging consumers exactly what they are willing to pay. Methods:

1. **Skimming pricing** — high entry price that falls over time.
2. **Penetration pricing** — low entry price that rises over time.

### Competition-Based Pricing

Setting prices in relation to competitors' prices. Methods:

1. **Premium pricing**
2. **Discount pricing**

---

## Pricing Factors

Pricing decisions are influenced by **internal** and **external** factors.

| External | Internal |
|---|---|
| Consumers | Marketing purposes |
| State regulation | Marketing strategy |
| Intermediaries | Expenses |
| Competitors | Stage of product life cycle |

### Consumer Factor

Consumers strongly influence pricing decisions, explained by two economic principles:

- The law of demand and price elasticity of demand.
- Market segmentation — buyers in different segments react differently to price.

**Factors affecting demand for medical products:**

- Disease in the population
- Need for medical products
- Presence or absence of analogues
- Competitiveness of medical products
- The price of the preparation and its analogues
- Popularity of a medical product (including through advertising and information)
- Solvency (purchasing power) of consumers
- Consumer habits

### State Regulation Factor

The state intervenes in pricing through:

- Restricting price levels
- Introducing taxes to withdraw part of the income of manufacturers and consumers
- State support of prices through grants to manufacturers or consumers

### Competition Factor

Marketing experts analyze the competitive environment. Economists distinguish four market types:

**1. Pure competition**

- Many small buyers and sellers, each with complete market information.
- No single buyer or seller controls demand, supply or price.
- The product is homogeneous — every seller markets the same product.
- Easy to enter or leave the market.
- Often found in agricultural products such as cereal grains or fresh fruit and vegetables.

**2. Monopolistic competition**

- Many buyers and sellers, but they lack complete market information.
- Each seller tries to gain a differential advantage over competitors.
- *Examples: smartphones, devices, cars.*

**3. Oligopoly**

- Only a few sellers (of products that can be differentiated, but not greatly).
- Each seller has a high market share and cannot ignore the others' actions.
- *Examples: steel manufacturing, oil, railroads, pharmaceuticals.*

**4. Monopoly**

- One firm stands apart and rules the entire demand and supply chain in its field.
- *Examples: government-provided public transport such as railways, water and electricity; copyrights and patents.*

## Distribution

- **Distribution:** the company's activity of planning, organizing and controlling the physical movement of materials and finished products from their place of manufacture to the point of use, to meet consumer needs and earn profit.
- **Distribution channel:** a set of firms or individuals who take on, or help transfer, ownership of a product or service on its way from producer to consumer. Channel participants are also called **intermediaries**.

### Tasks of Distribution Policy

| Strategic tasks | Tactical tasks |
|---|---|
| 1. Planning (forecasting) future distribution channels | 1. Working with existing customers and attracting new ones |
| 2. Selecting systems and methods of marketing and related types of channels | 2. Searching for and selecting commercial offers for the supply of goods |
| 3. Routing of sales | 3. Organizing orders, including transportation and freight/cargo-handling operations |
| 4. Establishing a network of wholesale and retail outlets | 4. Ensuring the effectiveness of marketing |

### Functions of Marketing Channels

1. **Reporting:** collecting and processing information from market research for planning and organizing distribution.
2. **Promotion:** developing and spreading information about products through marketing communication to attract buyers.
3. **Contacts and negotiation:** building relationships with potential buyers; agreeing on prices, volumes and delivery terms.
4. **Ordering:** concluding contracts with other channel members to purchase products from the manufacturer, and monitoring them.
5. **Adapting products to customer requirements:** production, sorting, packing and wrapping, assembling, after-sales service.
6. **Physical movement of goods:** organizing transportation and warehousing.
7. **Financing:** finding and allocating the funds needed to cover the channel's operating costs.

### Choosing a Distribution Channel

- The choice depends on **costs and profits**.
- With external channels, both costs and benefits are shared among all channel participants.

## Types of Intermediaries in the Pharmaceutical Market

```
Intermediaries (wholesale enterprises)
├── Wholesale intermediaries
│   ├── Independent (own the goods): Distributors, Dealers
│   └── Dependent (do not own the goods): Brokers, Commissionaires,
│       Consignees, Wholesale (trade) agents
└── Retail intermediaries: Pharmacies and their subdivisions
```

### Independent Intermediaries (with ownership of goods)

**Wholesaler**

- A legal or natural person who receives large quantities of goods from different manufacturers and organizes their movement to retail or directly to consumers.
- Usually the owner of the goods; profit comes from the difference between buying and selling price.

**Distributor**

- A company or person mainly performing import operations, acting as a dealer under contracts with producers (sellers) and buyers.
- Buys and sells in its own name and at its own expense; usually located in the importing country.

**Dealer**

- An independent intermediary specializing in durable goods that need considerable after-sales service; operates in its own name and at its own expense.

**Retailer**

- A legal or natural person who sells goods directly to the final consumer, having acquired them from a wholesaler or manufacturer.

### Dependent Intermediaries (no ownership of goods sold)

**Broker**

- Takes part in the sale by bringing buyer and seller together; receives a commission (brokerage) from the completed deal.
- Does not buy goods in their own name and takes no responsibility for them.

**Commissionaire**

- Has a warehouse and, for a fee, sells goods for the benefit and at the expense of the owner, but in their own name.
- Paid as a percentage of the transaction, or the difference between the principal's set price and the sale price.

**Consignee**

- Differs from a commissionaire: takes the owner's goods into their warehouse and sells them in the owner's name but at their own expense.
- Paid as goods are sold from the warehouse, depending on the type of consignment (simple, partially used, returned).

**Wholesale agent**

- Works with the manufacturer under a specific agreement, operating at its own expense and on its own behalf; may be granted exclusive selling rights in some regions.
- Remuneration is set by the principal, usually a percentage of the deal.

# Pharmacoeconomics — Lecture 7: Introduction & Costs

*Dr. Asmaa Salah*

---

## Pharmacoeconomics

**Definition:** the description and analysis of the costs of drug therapy to the health-care system and society.

> "Pharmacoeconomics research identifies, measures and compares the cost and consequences of health-care interventions such as drugs, devices, procedures, etc."

Pharmacoeconomics is a branch of **health economics** that compares the value of one drug or drug therapy to another.

*Slide note (Egypt):* health spending rose from 37.2 billion in 2014 to 128.1 billion in 2023 (as stated on the slide).

### Aim of Pharmacoeconomics

- To compare the economics of **different pharmaceutical products**, or
- To compare **drug therapy to other** treatments.

### Importance of Pharmacoeconomics

1. Determines which drug should be included in the **formulary**, by choosing the most effective treatment at the lowest price.
2. Helps create **clinical guidelines** for physicians to prescribe the most efficient drug.
3. Compares different drugs directly to find the **most effective and efficient treatment at the least cost**.

---

## Inputs and Outcomes

| Input (economic resources) — Costs | Outcomes (health & economic consequences) |
|---|---|
| Price of drugs and their administration | Prolonged lifetime |
| Price of hospitalization | Improved quality of life |
| Price of transportation | Prolonged remission-free interval |
| Price of ADRs (adverse drug reactions) | Better compliance |

### Perspectives

Four perspectives: **Patient, Provider, Payer, Society**.

### Key Terms

- **Cost:** the value of the resources consumed by a programme or drug therapy of interest.
- **Consequences:** the effects, outputs or outcomes of the programme or therapy of interest.
- **Perspective:** whose costs are relevant, based on the purpose of the study.

---

## Principle of Pharmacoeconomics

### The Pharmacoeconomic Equation

> **Costs ← Rx → Outcomes (Consequences)**

- **Centre:** the drug product
- **Left side:** cost analysis
- **Right side:** outcome study
- For a true analysis, both sides must be compared.

---

## 1. Cost

Health-care costs are categorized as:

- Direct costs (medical and non-medical)
- Indirect costs
- Intangible costs
- Incremental costs
- Opportunity costs

### 1. Direct Medical Costs

Incurred for medical products and services to prevent or treat disease:

- Drugs, materials
- Laboratory tests
- Health-care professionals' time (doctor's fees)
- Hospitalization
- Medical supplies and equipment

### 2. Direct Non-Medical Costs

For non-medical services that result from disease but do not involve direct medical services:

- Transportation to and from health-care facilities
- Lodging and boarding near hospitals
- Food
- Child and family care expenses

### 3. Indirect Non-Medical Costs

Cost of reduced productivity:

- Morbidity cost (patient's lost wages due to illness)
- Loss of productivity
- Loss of years of service due to premature death

### 4. Intangible Costs

(Cannot be touched; no physical presence.) Non-financial outcomes of disease and medical care, not expressed in money:

- Pain
- Suffering
- Grief
- Depression

### 5. Incremental Costs

The extra cost required to purchase an additional unit of effect:

- Plastic surgery
- Behavioural therapy

### 6. Opportunity Costs

- The amount lost by not using economic resources in their best alternative use (labour, capital, buildings, management, etc.).
- Resources invested in one area come at the expense of losing another opportunity.
- The value (economic benefit) of the alternative therapy that was forgone.

### Cost Formulas

> **Direct costs** = Direct medical costs + Direct non-medical costs
>
> **Indirect costs** = Morbidity costs + Mortality costs
>
> **Total costs** = Direct costs + Indirect costs + Incremental costs + Intangible costs

---

## Three Methods for Valuing Indirect Costs

### 1. Human Capital Approach

- Measures the cost of lost productivity; also called the **foregone earnings approach**.
- If actual wages are unknown, it uses a sample of individuals to create a comparable earnings estimate for the group (by age and sex).
- The pure method does not count unpaid work losses (homemaking, child care, volunteer work).

### 2. Frictional Cost

- The cost of replacing the worker.

### 3. Willingness to Pay (WTP)

- Measures the benefit of an intervention as the maximum amount people are willing to pay for it (or to prevent adverse outcomes such as lost productivity), using a series of carefully phrased questions about payment.

# Pharmacoeconomics — Lecture 8: Outcomes, Perspectives & Evaluation Methods

*Dr. Asmaa Salah*

---

## Principle of Pharmacoeconomics (Review)

> **Costs ← Rx → Outcomes (Consequences)**

- **Centre:** the drug product
- **Left side:** cost analysis
- **Right side:** outcome study
- For a true analysis, both sides must be compared.

**Outcomes can be:**

- **Positive:** the desired effect of the drug
- **Negative:** adverse effects or toxicity of the drug

---

## 2. Consequences (Outcomes) — the ECHO Model

**E**conomic, **C**linical and **H**umanistic **O**utcomes → outcomes research.

### Economic Outcome

- Reduction in resource use
- Savings due to treatment (direct monetary benefit)
- Production gain from returning to work (indirect monetary benefit)

### Clinical Outcome

Medical events that occur as a result of disease or treatment (e.g., safety and efficacy end points):

- Laboratory values
- Number of lives saved
- Number of deaths prevented
- Number of disability days reduced

### Humanistic Outcome

Consequences of disease or treatment on the patient's functional status — physical function, social function, general health and well-being, and life satisfaction or quality of life — measured as:

- **Utility unit:** changes in health-related quality of life (HRQOL) measures.
- **Quality-adjusted life years (QALY):** a widely used measure that integrates both quality and quantity of life.

### Example

A treatment increases life expectancy by 2 years but causes adverse effects or inconvenience that reduce quality of life (utility) by 25%. Calculate the humanistic outcome.

**Answer:** Humanistic outcome (net gain) = 2 × 0.75 = **1.5 QALY**

---

## 3. Perspectives

- **Patient perspective:** the portion of cost not covered by insurance.
- **Provider perspective:** e.g., hospitals — direct costs.
- **Payer perspective:** e.g., insurance companies, employers or the government.
- **Society perspective:** all direct and indirect costs.

---

## Steps for Conducting a Pharmacoeconomic (PE) Analysis

1. **Define the problem.** Identify the disease state and the aspect you want to address. *E.g., what is the most cost-effective method for controlling glucose in type II diabetes?*
2. **Identify the perspective.** Who will use the information, and to make what decisions? This guides the choice of relevant costs and benefits.
3. **Identify alternative interventions.**
   - What are the relevant choices?
   - Often a head-to-head comparison of the most used (traditional) treatment with the new one.
   - Compare with the most likely substitute for a realistic result.
   - The comparator doesn't have to be a drug therapy.
4. **Identify and measure the outcomes of each intervention.** Typical outcomes:
   - Cure rate (percent cured)
   - Improved quality of life
   - Decreased incidence of morbidity
   - Years of extended life
   - Relief or reduction of symptoms
   - Adverse events (drug interactions and side effects)
5. **Identify, measure and value the costs.**
   - Direct medical costs — hospitalization and any treatment costs
   - Direct non-medical costs — transportation to get treatment
   - Indirect costs — missed work due to illness
   - Intangible costs — pain and suffering

---

## Sensitivity Analysis (SA)

- When estimating costs and outcomes, there is usually a range of possible values.
- SA recalculates the results at the different values to see whether the conclusion changes.
- By altering important variables and recalculating, SA **tests the validity of the conclusion**.
- Example questions:
  - Would agent A still be most cost-effective if agent B's effect were greater than measured in the clinical trial?
  - Would agent A still be most cost-effective if agent B's monitoring costs were actually lower?
- SA becomes more important the more assumptions are made.

---

## Pharmacoeconomic Evaluations

Any study designed to assess the costs (resources consumed) and consequences (clinical, humanistic) of alternative therapies.

**Models:**

- Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)
- Cost-minimization analysis (CMA)
- Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)
- Cost-utility analysis (CUA)
- Cost-of-illness analysis (COI)

| Method | Costs measured in | Outcomes measured in | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMA | Money | Assumed equivalent (not measured) | Least-cost option |
| CEA | Money | Natural units (cases cured, lives saved…) | CER = cost / effectiveness |
| CBA | Money | Money | Benefit : Cost ratio |
| CUA | Money | QALY / patient preference | CUR = cost / QALY |
| COI | Money | — (economic burden of a disease) | Baseline for comparison |

---

### Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)

- A series of analytical and mathematical procedures that help select a course of action from various alternatives.
- Used to compare two or more treatment options for a **specific condition** — e.g., in severe reflux oesophagitis, a proton-pump inhibitor vs. H2 blockers.
- Identifies, examines and compares the relevant costs and consequences of competing drug regimens and interventions.
- **Costs** are in monetary terms; **consequences** are in natural units: cases cured, lives saved, hospitalizations prevented.
- Helps the decision maker identify a preferred choice among alternatives.

**Formulas:**

> CER = Cost / Effectiveness
>
> Average cost-effectiveness ratio (ACER) = Net cost / Net health benefit
>
> Incremental CER = (Cost of drug A − Cost of drug B) / (Benefit of drug A − Benefit of drug B)

### Cost-Minimization Analysis (CMA)

- The simplest method.
- Compares the costs of two or more alternatives with **demonstrated equivalence in therapeutic outcome**.
- Costs are in money; outcomes are assumed equivalent (not measured).
- Objective: select the **least costly** of several equivalent drugs.
- *Example:* if drugs A and B are antiulcer agents equivalent in efficacy and ADRs, their costs can be compared with CMA.

**Examples of CMA:**

- Brand vs. generic products
- Different routes of administration of the same drug

| | Drug A | Drug B |
|---|---|---|
| **Acquisition cost** | 250 | 350 |
| **Antibiotic effectiveness** | 90% | 90% |

→ Equal outcomes, so the cheaper option (Drug A) is preferred.

### Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)

- Measures **both costs and benefits in monetary terms**.
- Results are expressed as a **benefit-to-cost (B:C) ratio**.
- Many CBAs measure only direct costs and direct benefits.
- Allows comparisons across a wide variety of alternatives.

### Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA)

- Compares treatments or programmes where costs are in money and outcomes are in terms of **patient preference or quality of life (QALY)**.
- Result is a **cost-utility ratio (C:U)**:

> CUR = Cost / QALY (the lower, the more preferred)

- **QALY:** an assessment of the functional effects of illness and its therapy as perceived by the patient — physical, emotional and social effects — often gathered by questionnaires.
- CUA is used less often than other methods because of disagreement on measuring utilities and difficulty comparing QALYs across patients and populations.

### Cost-of-Illness Analysis (COI)

- Measures the **economic burden of a disease** and estimates the maximum amount that could be saved or gained if the disease were eradicated.
- Provides a **baseline** against which prevention or treatment options can be compared.
- Often used for infectious diseases.

## Example 1 — Standardization Cost Value

| Medical resource | Units of each resource | Cost per unit |
|---|---|---|
| Doctor visits | 8 visits | $20 per 2 visits |
| Medications | 28 capsules | $1.05 per 4 capsules |
| Hospitalization | 7 nights | $50 per night |

**Question:** What is the total standardization cost value?

### Answer

- **Doctor visits:** 8 ÷ 2 = 4 units → 4 × 20 = **$80**
- **Medication:** 28 ÷ 4 = 7 units → 7 × 1.05 = **$7.35**
- **Hospitalization:** 7 × 50 = **$350**

**Total standardization cost value = 80 + 7.35 + 350 = $437.35**

## Example 2 — Choosing CMA vs. CEA

| Medication | Cidophage | Glucophage | Glimepiride |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dosage form | Tablet | Tablet | Tablet |
| Strength | 1 g | 1 g | 1 mg |
| Cost (LE) | 18 | 35 | 27 |
| Effectiveness | Lowers fasting blood glucose by an average of 25% | Lowers fasting blood glucose by an average of 25% | Lowers fasting blood glucose by an average of 15% |

**Questions:**

1. Which analysis can be used to compare medications 1 & 2? Why?
2. Which analysis is suitable to compare medications 1 & 3?
3. Apply this method to them.

### Answer

1. **CMA** — the two medications are equivalent (same outcome), so we simply compare cost.
2. **CEA** — the two medications have the same type of outcome (lowering blood glucose) but different effectiveness.
3. CER = Difference in costs (1 & 3) / Difference in benefits (1 & 3) = (18 − 27) / (25 − 15)

   *The slide gives the result as −15.36; the arithmetic gives **−9 / 10 = −0.9 LE per 1% reduction**. Either way, the result is negative.*

**Interpretation:** a **negative** incremental CER means one treatment — the **dominant** option — is both **more effective and less expensive** than the other (the **dominated** option). Here, Cidophage dominates Glimepiride.

## Example 3 — Cost-Benefit Analysis

| Type of cost | Treatment A | Treatment B |
|---|---|---|
| Doctor visits | $10 | $15 |
| Cost of IV supplies | $5 | $7 |
| Cost of drug | $30 | $20 |
| Cost of transportation | $15 | $40 |
| **Total benefits** | **$30** | **$35** |

**Question:** Calculate the CBA for treatments A & B.

### Answer

- Total cost of treatment A = 10 + 5 + 30 + 15 = **$60**
- Total cost of treatment B = 15 + 7 + 20 + 40 = **$82**
- Benefit (B − A) / Cost (B − A) = (35 − 30) / (82 − 60) = 5 / 22 = **0.23**

**Decision rule:**

- B/C ratio **> 1** → the intervention is beneficial.
- B/C ratio **≤ 1** → not beneficial.

Here, 0.23 < 1, so the extra cost of treatment B over A is **not beneficial**.
