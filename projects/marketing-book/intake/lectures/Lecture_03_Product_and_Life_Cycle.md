## The Product in Marketing Activity

### Definition of Goods

- Goods are the product of work made for sale on the market to satisfy a certain need. So a product (or good) is a **physical thing or a service**.
- Consumers buy products that include: **variety, quality, design, features, brand name, packaging, services** — for the satisfaction of needs and the benefit they expect to receive.

### Difference Between Goods and Services

| Feature | Goods | Services |
|---|---|---|
| Tangible | ✓ | ✗ |
| Lag between production and consumption | ✓ | ✗ |
| Storage | ✓ | ✗ |

| Transfer (of ownership) | ✓ | ✗ |

## Classifications of Consumer Goods

1. **Daily demand** — goods that satisfy basic daily needs and emergencies. *E.g., food, water, clothes.*
2. **Previous choice** — goods that the consumer, while choosing and buying, compares on suitability, quality, price and appearance. *E.g., watches, handbags, mobiles.*
3. **Special demand** — products with unique characteristics, for which a considerable share of buyers is ready to make extra effort. *E.g., drugs for a patient, air conditioners in hot countries, cars for transport.*
4. **Passive demand** — products the buyer does not know about, or knows about but mostly does not think of buying. *E.g., energy-efficiency measures, engines.*

### By Durability / Material Tangibility

- **Long-use (durable) goods:** not consumed or destroyed by use; last a long time — usable for at least three years. *E.g., office furniture, electronics, motorcycles, jewellery.*
- **Short-term (non-durable) goods:** short lifespan; usually consumed in one use or after a short period. *E.g., food, cosmetics, laundry detergent.*

### Durable vs. Non-Durable Goods

| Durable goods | Non-durable goods |
|---|---|
| Hard goods | Soft goods |
| Used for a long period of time | Used for a short period of time only |
| Examples: cars, books, television, freezer, etc. | Examples: petrol, cosmetics, frozen items, soaps, etc. |
| Can be used many times | Can be used only a limited number of times, in some cases only once |
| Can be resold after some years | Resale opportunity does not exist |

## Pharmaceutical Goods

Pharmaceutical goods are a special group divided into two subgroups: **medical products** and **products of medical appointment (PMA)**.

### Pharmaceuticals (Medicines)

Any substance or combination of substances (in a certain medicinal form) intended for the treatment or prevention of human disease, for diagnosis, or for the restoration, correction or change of physiological functions — packed and placed on the market.

### Classification of Medical Products

- **Anatomical principle** — preparations acting on the respiratory, cardiovascular, digestive system, etc.
- **Therapeutic application** — analgesics, antibiotics, hypotensive agents, etc.
- **Pharmacological action** — diuretics, anticoagulants, neuroleptics, etc.
- **Chemical structure** — alkaloids, cardiac glycosides, hormonal agents, etc.
- **Kind of control and circulation order** — narcotics, psychotropic substances.
- **Prescription and non-prescription drugs.**
- **Direction of use** — internal, external.
- **Physical and chemical properties and environmental sensitivity** — photosensitive, thermolabile, dyes, etc.
- **Kind of dosage form** — solutions, suspensions, tablets, ointments, etc.
- **Ready-made drugs (industrial production) vs. individually compounded medicines.**

### Products of Medical Appointment (PMA)

Medical-technical products, materials and medical products intended for the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of disease in the human body and/or support of these processes.

**Criteria for classifying PMA:**

- **By material** — rubber, glass, plastic products, etc.
- **By assortment group** — dressings, perfumery-cosmetic products, sanitary and hygiene items, etc.
- **By functional purpose** — patient-care items, medical tools, devices and equipment.
- **By storage group** — rubber products, dressings and auxiliary materials, plastic products, medical-technique products.

## Consumer Value

**Consumer value** is how much customers perceive your products or services to be worth. Customers must believe the product holds greater value than its price.

> **Consumer value (CV) = Perceived customer benefit − Total customer cost**

If perceived benefit > total cost → better customer satisfaction, more purchases, more profit.

### Types of Consumer Value

- **Functional value:** what an offer does — the solution it provides to the customer.
- **Monetary value:** the price paid relative to the offering's perceived worth; invites a trade-off between other values and monetary cost.
- **Social value:** how far owning a product or using a service lets the consumer connect with others.
- **Psychological value:** how far a product lets consumers express themselves or feel better.

### Properties of Goods That Achieve Consumer Value

- Quality — reliability, suitability, design
- Price matching quality
- Accompanying documentation
- Quality after-sales service
- A variety of assortment (opportunity of choice)

### Properties of Medical Products That Achieve Consumer Value

- Therapeutic efficiency
- Safety
- Convenience of application
- Profitability

## The Product Life Cycle (PLC)

- Products tend to go through different stages.
- The length of a product's life cycle is not fixed; it can last from weeks to years depending on the product.
- Four stages: **Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline** (sales volume plotted against time).

### 1. Introduction

**Definition:** the period during which initial market acceptance may be in doubt — often a period of slow growth.

**Properties:**

- Profits are often non-existent because of high marketing and other expenses.
- Marketing strategy is based on different combinations of product, price, promotion and distribution.
- Production costs, marketing costs and prices are traditionally high.
- Unless the product can be made efficiently with existing equipment despite unpredictable sales, mass-production economies are hard to achieve.

**In the pharmaceutical market:**

- The market consists of a small percentage of the medical population who are normally first to try new drugs and are influential with colleagues.
- If the product is as effective and safe as this core group wishes, word filters down to the rest of the medical community.
- Direct competition may not be a problem, but if the product merely does a job in a new or better way, it may be hard to convince physicians of the value of the change.

### 2. Growth

**Strategy:**

- (a) Product improvement — new features and models
- (b) Development of new market segments
- (c) Selective demand stimulation
- (d) Price adjustments

**Properties:**

- Substantial improvement in profits.
- Widespread approval of the product concept.
- The number of competitors begins to increase.
- Modifications of the original product, or completely different products for the same purpose, appear.
- Production methods are established, often with lower costs.
- Prices tend to fall for two reasons:
  1. Increased sales make economies of scale possible.
  2. More competing firms push prices down, in theory and in practice.

### 3. Maturity

Sales growth slows and profits peak, then begin to decline.

**Strategy:**

- (a) Search for new markets and new, varied uses for the product
- (b) Improve product quality through changes
- (c) New marketing-mix perspectives

**In the pharmaceutical market:**

- All product variations appear: tablets, capsules, liquid, a dermatologic form, a long-acting form, or a combination.
- Promotion tries to add vitality by segmenting the market with special messages for different physician specialties.

**Properties:**

- Competition reaches its peak.
- All firms hoping for market share are pursuing it; some are marginal financially or technologically.
- Total sales of the product class keep rising, but at a decreasing rate — the net effect is price competition.

### 4. Decline

**Properties:**

- A declining product is not necessarily unprofitable.
- Reasons for decline include:
  - The product's effectiveness compared with other therapies.
  - If caused by a new product, decline may be rapid — only those slow to change continue to prescribe it.
- Promotion may again aim at stimulating primary demand.
- The effort may be half-hearted, aimed only at a core of physician-users.
- Some firms drop out, leaving an opportunity for those remaining.

## PLC Summary Tables

### A. Introduction Stage

| | |
|---|---|
| Sales grow slowly | Profit is minimal or negative |
| Create awareness | Stimulate trial |
| High production costs | Limited product models |
| Frequent product modification | Penetration pricing |
| Skimming pricing | Little competition |
| High failure rate | High marketing costs |
| Developing product awareness | Informing about product benefits |

- Intensive personal selling to retailers and wholesalers is required.
- Promotion strategy focuses on primary demand for the product category.

### B. Growth Stage

| | |
|---|---|
| Sales grow at an increasing rate | Many competitors enter the market |
| Large companies may acquire small pioneering firms | Profits are healthy |
| Heavy brand advertising | Differences between brands |
| Development costs have been recovered | Sales volume has created economies of scale |

- Promotion emphasis.
- Gaining wider distribution is a key goal.
- **Toward the end of this stage:** prices normally fall and profits reach their peak.

### C. Maturity Stage

| | |
|---|---|
| Sales continue to increase but at a decreasing rate | The marketplace is approaching saturation |
| Annual models of many products | Emphasis on product style rather than function |
| Product lines are widened or extended | Marginal competitors begin dropping out of the market |
| Heavy promotions to both dealers and consumers are required | Prices and profits begin to fall |

### D. Decline Stage

| | |
|---|---|
| Signalled by a long-run drop in sales | Falling demand forces many competitors out of the market |
| The rate of decline depends on how rapidly consumer tastes change or substitutes are adopted | A few small specialty firms may still manufacture the product |

**Strategies:**

- **Deletion** — dropping the product from the company's product line; the most drastic strategy.
- **Harvesting** — the company keeps the product but reduces marketing support.

### To Prevent Slipping Into Decline

- Promote more frequent use of the product by current customers
- Find new target markets for the product
- Find new uses for the product
- Price the product below the market
- Develop new distribution channels
- Add new ingredients
- Delete old ingredients
- Make a dramatic new guarantee
