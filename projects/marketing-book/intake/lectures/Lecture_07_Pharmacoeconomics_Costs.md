## Pharmacoeconomics

**Definition:** the description and analysis of the costs of drug therapy to the health-care system and society.

> "Pharmacoeconomics research identifies, measures and compares the cost and consequences of health-care interventions such as drugs, devices, procedures, etc."

Pharmacoeconomics is a branch of **health economics** that compares the value of one drug or drug therapy to another.

*Slide note (Egypt):* health spending rose from 37.2 billion in 2014 to 128.1 billion in 2023 (as stated on the slide).

### Aim of Pharmacoeconomics

- To compare the economics of **different pharmaceutical products**, or
- To compare **drug therapy to other** treatments.

### Importance of Pharmacoeconomics

1. Determines which drug should be included in the **formulary**, by choosing the most effective treatment at the lowest price.
2. Helps create **clinical guidelines** for physicians to prescribe the most efficient drug.
3. Compares different drugs directly to find the **most effective and efficient treatment at the least cost**.

## Inputs and Outcomes

| Input (economic resources) — Costs | Outcomes (health & economic consequences) |
|---|---|
| Price of drugs and their administration | Prolonged lifetime |
| Price of hospitalization | Improved quality of life |
| Price of transportation | Prolonged remission-free interval |
| Price of ADRs (adverse drug reactions) | Better compliance |

### Perspectives

Four perspectives: **Patient, Provider, Payer, Society**.

### Key Terms

- **Cost:** the value of the resources consumed by a programme or drug therapy of interest.
- **Consequences:** the effects, outputs or outcomes of the programme or therapy of interest.
- **Perspective:** whose costs are relevant, based on the purpose of the study.

## Principle of Pharmacoeconomics

### The Pharmacoeconomic Equation

> **Costs ← Rx → Outcomes (Consequences)**

- **Centre:** the drug product
- **Left side:** cost analysis
- **Right side:** outcome study
- For a true analysis, both sides must be compared.

## 1. Cost

Health-care costs are categorized as:

- Direct costs (medical and non-medical)
- Indirect costs
- Intangible costs
- Incremental costs
- Opportunity costs

### 1. Direct Medical Costs

Incurred for medical products and services to prevent or treat disease:

- Drugs, materials
- Laboratory tests
- Health-care professionals' time (doctor's fees)
- Hospitalization
- Medical supplies and equipment

### 2. Direct Non-Medical Costs

For non-medical services that result from disease but do not involve direct medical services:

- Transportation to and from health-care facilities
- Lodging and boarding near hospitals
- Food
- Child and family care expenses

### 3. Indirect Non-Medical Costs

Cost of reduced productivity:

- Morbidity cost (patient's lost wages due to illness)
- Loss of productivity
- Loss of years of service due to premature death

### 4. Intangible Costs

(Cannot be touched; no physical presence.) Non-financial outcomes of disease and medical care, not expressed in money:

- Pain
- Suffering
- Grief
- Depression

### 5. Incremental Costs

The extra cost required to purchase an additional unit of effect:

- Plastic surgery
- Behavioural therapy

### 6. Opportunity Costs

- The amount lost by not using economic resources in their best alternative use (labour, capital, buildings, management, etc.).
- Resources invested in one area come at the expense of losing another opportunity.
- The value (economic benefit) of the alternative therapy that was forgone.

### Cost Formulas

> **Direct costs** = Direct medical costs + Direct non-medical costs
>
> **Indirect costs** = Morbidity costs + Mortality costs
>
> **Total costs** = Direct costs + Indirect costs + Incremental costs + Intangible costs

## Three Methods for Valuing Indirect Costs

### 1. Human Capital Approach

- Measures the cost of lost productivity; also called the **foregone earnings approach**.
- If actual wages are unknown, it uses a sample of individuals to create a comparable earnings estimate for the group (by age and sex).
- The pure method does not count unpaid work losses (homemaking, child care, volunteer work).

### 2. Frictional Cost

- The cost of replacing the worker.

### 3. Willingness to Pay (WTP)

- Measures the benefit of an intervention as the maximum amount people are willing to pay for it (or to prevent adverse outcomes such as lost productivity), using a series of carefully phrased questions about payment.

## Activity

A worker fell while working, felt severe pain in his left foot and could not stand on it. He took a taxi to the nearby primary-care unit — he could have gone to the large hospital in the city centre, but didn't. He paid the admission ticket and asked the doctor for X-rays to check for a fracture. There was a fracture in his left foot, so he had it fixed. The doctor examined him and told him to rest at home for a month, and to take certain medications and foods that help healing. The worker felt sadness and suffering because he would lose part of his wages and had no one to care for him. He bought crutches to help him move, and he had to provide an alternative worker to do his job during this period.

**Question:** What types of costs will this worker incur until he is cured?
