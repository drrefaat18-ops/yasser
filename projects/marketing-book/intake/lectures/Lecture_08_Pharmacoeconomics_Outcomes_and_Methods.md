## Principle of Pharmacoeconomics (Review)

> **Costs ← Rx → Outcomes (Consequences)**

- **Centre:** the drug product
- **Left side:** cost analysis
- **Right side:** outcome study
- For a true analysis, both sides must be compared.

**Outcomes can be:**

- **Positive:** the desired effect of the drug
- **Negative:** adverse effects or toxicity of the drug

---

## 2. Consequences (Outcomes) — the ECHO Model

**E**conomic, **C**linical and **H**umanistic **O**utcomes → outcomes research.

### Economic Outcome

- Reduction in resource use
- Savings due to treatment (direct monetary benefit)
- Production gain from returning to work (indirect monetary benefit)

### Clinical Outcome

Medical events that occur as a result of disease or treatment (e.g., safety and efficacy end points):

- Laboratory values
- Number of lives saved
- Number of deaths prevented
- Number of disability days reduced

### Humanistic Outcome

Consequences of disease or treatment on the patient's functional status — physical function, social function, general health and well-being, and life satisfaction or quality of life — measured as:

- **Utility unit:** changes in health-related quality of life (HRQOL) measures.
- **Quality-adjusted life years (QALY):** a widely used measure that integrates both quality and quantity of life.

### Example

A treatment increases life expectancy by 2 years but causes adverse effects or inconvenience that reduce quality of life (utility) by 25%. Calculate the humanistic outcome.

**Answer:** Humanistic outcome (net gain) = 2 × 0.75 = **1.5 QALY**

## 3. Perspectives

- **Patient perspective:** the portion of cost not covered by insurance.
- **Provider perspective:** e.g., hospitals — direct costs.
- **Payer perspective:** e.g., insurance companies, employers or the government.
- **Society perspective:** all direct and indirect costs.

## Steps for Conducting a Pharmacoeconomic (PE) Analysis

1. **Define the problem.** Identify the disease state and the aspect you want to address. *E.g., what is the most cost-effective method for controlling glucose in type II diabetes?*
2. **Identify the perspective.** Who will use the information, and to make what decisions? This guides the choice of relevant costs and benefits.
3. **Identify alternative interventions.**
   - What are the relevant choices?
   - Often a head-to-head comparison of the most used (traditional) treatment with the new one.
   - Compare with the most likely substitute for a realistic result.
   - The comparator doesn't have to be a drug therapy.
4. **Identify and measure the outcomes of each intervention.** Typical outcomes:
   - Cure rate (percent cured)
   - Improved quality of life
   - Decreased incidence of morbidity
   - Years of extended life
   - Relief or reduction of symptoms
   - Adverse events (drug interactions and side effects)
5. **Identify, measure and value the costs.**
   - Direct medical costs — hospitalization and any treatment costs
   - Direct non-medical costs — transportation to get treatment
   - Indirect costs — missed work due to illness
   - Intangible costs — pain and suffering

---

## Sensitivity Analysis (SA)

- When estimating costs and outcomes, there is usually a range of possible values.
- SA recalculates the results at the different values to see whether the conclusion changes.
- By altering important variables and recalculating, SA **tests the validity of the conclusion**.
- Example questions:
  - Would agent A still be most cost-effective if agent B's effect were greater than measured in the clinical trial?
  - Would agent A still be most cost-effective if agent B's monitoring costs were actually lower?
- SA becomes more important the more assumptions are made.

## Pharmacoeconomic Evaluations

Any study designed to assess the costs (resources consumed) and consequences (clinical, humanistic) of alternative therapies.

**Models:**

- Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)
- Cost-minimization analysis (CMA)
- Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)
- Cost-utility analysis (CUA)
- Cost-of-illness analysis (COI)

| Method | Costs measured in | Outcomes measured in | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMA | Money | Assumed equivalent (not measured) | Least-cost option |
| CEA | Money | Natural units (cases cured, lives saved…) | CER = cost / effectiveness |
| CBA | Money | Money | Benefit : Cost ratio |
| CUA | Money | QALY / patient preference | CUR = cost / QALY |
| COI | Money | — (economic burden of a disease) | Baseline for comparison |

---

### Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)

- A series of analytical and mathematical procedures that help select a course of action from various alternatives.
- Used to compare two or more treatment options for a **specific condition** — e.g., in severe reflux oesophagitis, a proton-pump inhibitor vs. H2 blockers.
- Identifies, examines and compares the relevant costs and consequences of competing drug regimens and interventions.
- **Costs** are in monetary terms; **consequences** are in natural units: cases cured, lives saved, hospitalizations prevented.
- Helps the decision maker identify a preferred choice among alternatives.

**Formulas:**

> CER = Cost / Effectiveness
>
> Average cost-effectiveness ratio (ACER) = Net cost / Net health benefit
>
> Incremental CER = (Cost of drug A − Cost of drug B) / (Benefit of drug A − Benefit of drug B)

### Cost-Minimization Analysis (CMA)

- The simplest method.
- Compares the costs of two or more alternatives with **demonstrated equivalence in therapeutic outcome**.
- Costs are in money; outcomes are assumed equivalent (not measured).
- Objective: select the **least costly** of several equivalent drugs.
- *Example:* if drugs A and B are antiulcer agents equivalent in efficacy and ADRs, their costs can be compared with CMA.

**Examples of CMA:**

- Brand vs. generic products
- Different routes of administration of the same drug

| | Drug A | Drug B |
|---|---|---|
| **Acquisition cost** | 250 | 350 |
| **Antibiotic effectiveness** | 90% | 90% |

→ Equal outcomes, so the cheaper option (Drug A) is preferred.

### Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA)

- Measures **both costs and benefits in monetary terms**.
- Results are expressed as a **benefit-to-cost (B:C) ratio**.
- Many CBAs measure only direct costs and direct benefits.
- Allows comparisons across a wide variety of alternatives.

### Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA)

- Compares treatments or programmes where costs are in money and outcomes are in terms of **patient preference or quality of life (QALY)**.
- Result is a **cost-utility ratio (C:U)**:

> CUR = Cost / QALY (the lower, the more preferred)

- **QALY:** an assessment of the functional effects of illness and its therapy as perceived by the patient — physical, emotional and social effects — often gathered by questionnaires.
- CUA is used less often than other methods because of disagreement on measuring utilities and difficulty comparing QALYs across patients and populations.

### Cost-of-Illness Analysis (COI)

- Measures the **economic burden of a disease** and estimates the maximum amount that could be saved or gained if the disease were eradicated.
- Provides a **baseline** against which prevention or treatment options can be compared.
- Often used for infectious diseases.
