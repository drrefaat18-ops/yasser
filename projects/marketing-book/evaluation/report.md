## What the source is

Nine lectures of a pharmacy-faculty course: six on pharmaceutical marketing (the marketing concept and mix, digital
marketing and the pharmaceutical market, the product and its life cycle, pricing, distribution, marketing
communications) and three on pharmacoeconomics (costs; outcomes, perspectives and methods; worked applications).
They are slide notes, not a book: definitions, lists and tables, with a handful of worked examples in the economics
lectures. The score is low mainly because the notes were never meant to stand alone, and because this book's readers
are not the notes' readers.

## What the source gets right

The syllabus is the right one for an introductory course. The order — concept, market, the four elements of the
mix, communications, then the economics of care — is the order a textbook would use. The economics lectures cover
the standard ground correctly at the level of definitions: the cost categories, the ECHO model, the four
perspectives, the five evaluation methods and their units (the method table in lecture 8 is accurate), and
sensitivity analysis. The arithmetic reproduces: the micro-costing example (437.35), the QALY example (1.5,
given its unstated assumption), the corrected ICER (−0.9) and the incremental benefit-cost ratio (5 ÷ 22 = 0.23).
What is wrong is mostly the economics around the arithmetic: definitions and decision rules.

## What must change

**Audience (D1, the one blocker).** Every example is a medicine, a pharmacy or a pharmaceutical company. The readers
are third-year Applied Health Sciences students who will work in laboratories and imaging centres; laboratory tests and X-rays appear only as cost
items in the economics lectures, and no marketing concept is ever applied to either service. This is the central task of the rework: health services first, medicines as
one case.

**Services marketing (D1).** Health care is mostly a service, but only the 4 Ps are taught. People, process and
physical evidence are what a patient actually experiences in a laboratory or an imaging centre.

**Ethics and regulation (D1).** Sales promotion, lotteries, coupons, free samples and "neutralising" competitors'
advertising are presented without a single limit, and advertising is said to "expand the scope" of a medicine to new
indications. Public advertising of prescription medicines is prohibited in
Egypt as in most countries, and industry codes restrict inducements. The rubric makes ethics half of D1 for this
reason, and the template requires an Ethics Check in every chapter.

**Economics (G1, D2).** The errors are in definitions and decision rules, not in arithmetic. Incremental cost is
defined as a cost per unit of effect and illustrated with procedures; the total-cost formula adds incremental cost as
a category; the cost activity calls an unused hospital an opportunity cost; willingness to pay is listed as a way of
valuing indirect costs; the patient perspective is reduced to uninsured bills. On the evaluation side, the ACER is
defined as a ratio of net differences; average cost per QALY is used to rank options; CMA is applied without
demonstrated equivalence; a negative ICER is taught as dominance; Example 2 has no cost period or time horizon; and
the QALY is defined as a functional assessment rather than time weighted by utility. Price itself is defined as an
equivalent of cost, and formulary choice as "the most effective at the lowest price". Price elasticity is confused
with the law of demand, then contradicted by the next lecture.

**Terminology (G1).** Several terms are literal translations with no currency in English-language marketing
("products of medical appointment", "previous choice", "passive demand", "registration function of price").
Students cannot look them up.

**Sources (G4).** Nothing is cited. The definition of marketing is the AMA's 1985 one, replaced in 2007. An
Egyptian spending figure has no currency, definition or source.

**Teaching design (G2, G3).** No objectives, no summaries, no explanations, repeated reviews, diagrams referred to
but missing, and no questions at all in the six marketing lectures.

## For the design

The rework should keep every topic and every worked example, in order, and add what a service-focused book needs:
the 7 Ps; patients as consumers who often neither decide nor pay in the way a consumer does; ethics and regulation
within each topic; and a laboratory and an imaging lens throughout. The economics should be recast as health
economics, with laboratory and imaging comparisons alongside the medicine examples, and every calculation checked by
the harness math gate.
