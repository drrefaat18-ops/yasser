# Chapter 12: Applying Economic Evaluation

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:
1. [LO1] Calculate the total cost of a course of care by micro-costing.
2. [LO2] Choose between cost-minimisation and cost-effectiveness analysis for a given pair of options, and justify the choice.
3. [LO3] Calculate CERs and an ICER and locate the result on the cost-effectiveness plane.
4. [LO4] Calculate simple and incremental benefit–cost ratios and net benefit, and apply the decision rules.
5. [LO5] Apply economic evaluation to a laboratory or imaging decision and report it honestly.

## 12.1 Where Economic Evaluation Is Used

Economic evaluation supports three kinds of decision (Drummond et al., 2015):

1. *Assessing the value of a new product or service*, such as a new medicine, test or scanner.
2. *Pricing*, where evidence of value supports a fair price for a new product.
3. *Clinical and policy decisions*, including formulary and coverage decisions, treatment choices, medication policy and the allocation of budgets.

This chapter works through examples that use the methods of Chapters 10 and 11. Each calculation is shown step by step. All prices and effects are illustrative.

## 12.2 Micro-Costing

**Micro-costing** builds the cost of care from each resource used: the number of units of each resource times its unit cost, summed. Older lecture notes call the result a "standardisation cost value". The standard term is the total cost from resource use.

> **Worked Example:** Micro-costing a course of care
>
> A patient's course of care uses the resources below. The unit costs are given in US dollars, as in the source example, and some are priced per group of units.
>
> | Resource | Units used | Price |
> |---|---|---|
> | Doctor visits | 8 visits | $20 per 2 visits |
> | Medicine | 28 capsules | $1.05 per 4 capsules |
> | Hospital stay | 7 nights | $50 per night |
>
> *Step 1 — convert each resource to priced units.* Doctor visits: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 priced units. Medicine: 28 ÷ 4 = 7 priced units. Hospital stay: 7 nights.
>
> *Step 2 — cost each resource.* Doctor visits: 4 × $20 = $80. Medicine: 7 × $1.05 = $7.35. Hospital stay: 7 × $50 = $350.
>
> *Step 3 — sum.* Total cost = 80 + 7.35 + 350 = $437.35.
>
> *Check.* The hospital stay is 80% of the total, 350 ÷ 437.35 = 0.80. A cost analysis that ignores the stay would miss most of the cost.

## 12.3 Choosing Between CMA and CEA

The method follows the outcome. Chapter 11 set the rule: CMA only when equivalence is shown; CEA when outcomes are of the same kind but differ in size.

> **Worked Example:** Three medicines for type 2 diabetes
>
> Three tablets are compared for lowering fasting blood glucose. The costs and effects are illustrative, as in the source example, with the product names replaced by their generic descriptions. Costs are per patient for a 30-day supply; effects are the average fall in fasting glucose after three months of treatment. Without such a stated basis, an ICER cannot be interpreted.
>
> | Medicine | Strength | Cost (EGP) | Average fall in fasting glucose |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1. Metformin, generic brand | 1 g | 18 | 25% |
> | 2. Metformin, originator brand | 1 g | 35 | 25% |
> | 3. Glimepiride | 1 mg | 27 | 15% |
>
> *Question (a): medicines 1 and 2.* Same active ingredient, same strength, same effect. If their equivalence has been shown in every relevant outcome, including safety, the right method is CMA, and only cost matters. One equal glucose end point is not proof of equivalence by itself. Medicine 1 is preferred and saves 35 − 18 = 17 EGP.
>
> *Question (b): medicines 1 and 3.* Same kind of outcome, a fall in glucose, but different sizes. The right method is CEA.
>
> *Step 1 — CER of each.* Medicine 1: 18 ÷ 25 = 0.72 EGP per percentage point. Medicine 3: 27 ÷ 15 = 1.8 EGP per percentage point.
>
> *Step 2 — ICER of medicine 1 over medicine 3.* ΔC = 18 − 27 = −9 EGP. ΔE = 25 − 15 = 10 percentage points. ICER = −9 ÷ 10 = −0.9 EGP per percentage point.
>
> *Step 3 — locate it on the plane.* Medicine 1 costs 9 EGP less and lowers glucose by 10 points more. It falls in the south-east quadrant, so medicine 1 *dominates* medicine 3.
>
> *Correction to the source.* The source slide printed the ICER as −15.36. The arithmetic gives −0.9. The conclusion, dominance, is right, but it follows from the quadrant: one option is both cheaper and more effective. It does not follow from the negative sign alone.

> **Key Formula:** The ratios of this chapter
>
> $$ICER = \frac{C_A - C_B}{E_A - E_B}$$
>
> $$\frac{B}{C} = \frac{\text{total benefits}}{\text{total costs}} \qquad NB = B - C$$
>
> $$\text{incremental } \frac{B}{C} = \frac{B_A - B_B}{C_A - C_B}$$

## 12.4 Cost-Benefit Analysis

Cost-benefit analysis values both costs and benefits in money. It gives three results: each option's own benefit–cost ratio, its net benefit, and the incremental ratio between options. They answer different questions.

The decision rules for a benefit–cost ratio are:

- *greater than 1* — benefits exceed costs: a net gain;
- *equal to 1* — benefits equal costs: break-even;
- *less than 1* — costs exceed benefits: a net loss.

Net benefit gives the same verdict in money: positive, zero or negative.

> **Worked Example:** Two treatments compared by CBA
>
> Two treatments have the costs and benefits below, in US dollars as in the source example.
>
> | Cost item | Treatment A | Treatment B |
> |---|---|---|
> | Doctor visits | 10 | 15 |
> | Intravenous supplies | 5 | 7 |
> | Medicine | 30 | 20 |
> | Transport | 15 | 40 |
> | Total cost | 60 | 82 |
>
> | | Treatment A | Treatment B |
> |---|---|---|
> | Total benefits | 30 | 35 |
>
> *Step 1 — totals.* Cost of A = 10 + 5 + 30 + 15 = $60. Cost of B = 15 + 7 + 20 + 40 = $82.
>
> *Step 2 — each option's own ratio and net benefit.* A: B/C = 30 ÷ 60 = 0.50; net benefit = 30 − 60 = −$30. B: B/C = 35 ÷ 82 = 0.43; net benefit = 35 − 82 = −$47.
>
> *Step 3 — the incremental ratio of B over A.* (35 − 30) ÷ (82 − 60) = 5 ÷ 22 = 0.23.
>
> *Interpretation.* The incremental ratio, 0.23, is below 1: moving from A to B costs $22 more to gain $5 more. The extra cost of B is not justified. The source example stopped there. Step 2 adds a second finding: neither treatment returns its own cost in money, since both ratios are below 1. That may simply mean the study counted only some benefits. Health gains that were not valued in money are missing, which is common when only direct benefits are measured.

> **Common Mistake:** Reporting only the incremental ratio and calling a treatment "beneficial" or not. The incremental ratio compares two options; each option's own ratio says whether it is worth doing at all. Report both, and say which question each answers.

## 12.5 A Laboratory Application

> **Worked Example:** Cost per case detected for two screening tests
>
> A public programme screens 1,000 pregnant women for a treatable infection. The figures are illustrative.
>
> Test A, a rapid test: total cost 60,000 EGP; detects 18 cases. Test B, a laboratory method: total cost 90,000 EGP; detects 24 cases.
>
> *Step 1 — CERs.* A: 60,000 ÷ 18 = 3,333 EGP per case detected. B: 90,000 ÷ 24 = 3,750 EGP per case detected.
>
> *Step 2 — ICER of B over A.* (90,000 − 60,000) ÷ (24 − 18) = 30,000 ÷ 6 = 5,000 EGP per additional case detected.
>
> *Step 3 — interpret.* B is more costly and more effective: the north-east quadrant, a trade-off. The question is whether detecting one more case is worth 5,000 EGP. If treating an infection early avoids complications that cost more than that, B is worth it. The CERs alone would suggest A, but the decision rests on the ICER.
>
> *Sensitivity check.* If test B's cost fell to 78,000 EGP, the ICER would be (78,000 − 60,000) ÷ 6 = 3,000 EGP per additional case. The case for B becomes stronger.

> **Worked Example:** Cost per QALY of an imaging strategy
>
> A hospital compares two strategies for patients with a suspected condition, over the patients' remaining lifetime. The figures are illustrative, per patient, already discounted.
>
> Strategy A, current practice: cost 20,000 EGP; 8.00 QALYs. Strategy B, early MRI: cost 26,000 EGP; 8.20 QALYs.
>
> *Step 1 — differences.* ΔC = 26,000 − 20,000 = 6,000 EGP. ΔE = 8.20 − 8.00 = 0.20 QALY.
>
> *Step 2 — incremental cost per QALY.* 6,000 ÷ 0.20 = 30,000 EGP per QALY gained.
>
> *Step 3 — interpret.* B is more costly and more effective: the north-east quadrant. It is cost-effective only if the decision-maker is willing to pay at least 30,000 EGP for one QALY. The average ratios, 2,500 and about 3,171 EGP per QALY, would wrongly suggest A is always better, which is why average cost per QALY does not rank options.

> **Through Two Lenses**
> - **Medical Laboratory:** The screening example shows why laboratories should know cost per case detected, not only cost per test. A cheaper test that misses cases can be the more expensive choice once missed cases are counted. Laboratory staff supply the accuracy data such analyses depend on.
> - **Radiology and Medical Imaging:** Imaging decisions are often cost-utility questions, because scans change treatment and quality of life. A costlier scan that avoids unneeded surgery may give more QALYs at an acceptable cost. Imaging staff supply the diagnostic accuracy data such analyses depend on.

## 12.6 Reporting Honestly

> **Ethics Check:** A laboratory preparing a business case for a new analyser reports only the best-case volume and leaves out the sensitivity analysis. The case looks strong, and the board approves it. When volume falls short, the service loses money and cuts staff. Honest reporting shows the base case, the assumptions and the sensitivity analysis, including the cases where the conclusion changes (Husereau et al., 2022).

> **In Practice:** A hospital committee reviewing two imaging protocols asks for three things before deciding. It wants the perspective of the analysis, the source of every cost and effect, and the result when the most uncertain value changes by a plausible range. These three questions catch most weak economic evaluations.

> **Egyptian Context:** Economic evaluation in Egypt should use local costs in EGP and, where possible, local data on disease and outcomes. As purchasing under the universal health insurance system grows (Arab Republic of Egypt, 2018), laboratories and imaging centres that can document the value of their services will be better placed.

## Key Takeaways
- Micro-costing multiplies units by unit costs and sums them; the source's total, $437.35, is correct.
- Use CMA only when equivalence is shown; use CEA when outcomes differ in size.
- The source's ICER of −15.36 is wrong; the correct value is −0.9 EGP per percentage point.
- Dominance comes from the quadrant: cheaper and more effective.
- Report each option's own B/C and net benefit as well as the incremental B/C.
- A B/C ratio above 1 is a net gain, equal to 1 is break-even, below 1 is a net loss.
- Decisions rest on the ICER, tested by sensitivity analysis and reported honestly.

## Answers and Worked Solutions

**Q1. C** — 6 ÷ 2 = 3 priced units; 3 × 30 = 90 EGP. Option A multiplies all 6 visits by 30.

**Q2. D** — The stay costs $350 of $437.35, 80% of the total.

**Q3. A** — Demonstrated equivalence means only costs differ: CMA.

**Q4. C** — Same kind of outcome in different amounts calls for CEA.

**Q5. B** — (18 − 27) ÷ (25 − 15) = −9 ÷ 10 = −0.9. Option A is the source's misprint; options C and D are the CERs.

**Q6. A** — Cheaper and more effective is the south-east quadrant: the option dominates.

**Q7. D** — 35 ÷ 82 = 0.43. Option B is the incremental ratio from the example.

**Q8. D** — Benefits equal costs: break-even. The source's rule wrongly grouped 1 with a loss.

**Q9. C** — (90,000 − 60,000) ÷ (24 − 18) = 30,000 ÷ 6 = 5,000 EGP per additional case. Options A and B are the CERs.

**Q10. B** — Honest reporting shows the base case, assumptions and sensitivity analysis, including unfavourable results.

**E1 — model answer points.** Medicines 1 and 2 share ingredient, strength and effect: with equivalence shown, CMA; medicine 1 saves 17 EGP. Medicines 1 and 3 differ in effect: CEA. ICER = (18 − 27) ÷ (25 − 15) = −0.9 EGP per point; medicine 1 is cheaper and more effective, south-east, so it dominates. The source's −15.36 is a misprint.

**E2 — model answer points.** Costs 60 and 82. A: B/C 0.50, net benefit −30. B: B/C 0.43, net benefit −47. Incremental B/C 5 ÷ 22 = 0.23. Neither option returns its cost in money; B's extra cost is not justified by its extra benefit; unvalued health gains may be missing.

**E3 — model answer points.** State the perspective; define outcomes such as cases detected or QALYs; cost tests, confirmatory testing and consequences of missed cases; choose CEA or CUA; compute CERs and the ICER and locate it on the plane; vary uncertain values such as accuracy and price in sensitivity analysis; report all results.
