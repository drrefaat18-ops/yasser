## 9.1 Communicating With the Market

**Marketing communications** are the means by which an organisation informs, persuades and reminds its market about its products and itself (Kotler & Keller, 2016). **Promotion** is the part of marketing that builds and supports long-term relations with the market through such communication. Its goals are to activate sales and to create a positive image.

In health care, communication has a second, stricter goal: the patient and the professional must be told the truth. A misleading message about a medicine or a test can harm health. For this reason, promotion is the most regulated element of the health-care marketing mix.

## 9.2 Elements of Marketing Communications

The communication mix has main and supporting elements, as Table 9.1 shows.

| Main elements | Supporting elements |
|---|---|
| Advertising | Exhibitions and conferences |
| Personal selling | Branding |
| Public relations | Sponsorship |
| Sales promotion | Service policy |

*Table 9.1. Main and supporting elements of marketing communications.*

**Advertising** is paid, non-personal communication from an identified sponsor through media. **Personal selling** is face-to-face presentation, such as a representative's visit to a doctor. **Public relations** builds goodwill and reputation through news, community activity and information. **Sales promotion** offers short-term incentives to encourage purchase.

The supporting elements reinforce these. A laboratory's stand at a medical conference, its logo and brand, its sponsorship of a health awareness day, and its after-sales service all communicate.

### Purposes in health care

A health organisation communicates to:

1. draw the attention of professionals to a product or service;
2. inform them accurately about its properties and benefits;
3. support appropriate prescribing by doctors, or recommendation by pharmacists for over-the-counter products;
4. present its advantages honestly against competing products;
5. build a trustworthy brand image among professionals and patients.

The older lecture phrase "neutralise competitors' advertising" is better stated as the fourth purpose: answer competitors with accurate, comparative evidence, never with disparagement.

### Channels

Communication travels through personal channels, such as visits and professional meetings, and non-personal channels, such as print, broadcast, events and the setting of the service itself. Social media platforms and websites are now central. Chapter 3 covered them.

### What decides the choice of tools

Three factors decide which tools to use: the goals of the communication, the type of product, and the promotion strategy. A prescription medicine, a self-paid test and a hospital service call for different tools.

## 9.3 Push and Pull

Two strategies describe how promotion moves a product through its channel, as Figure 9.1 shows.

![](fig:ch09-push-pull)

A **push strategy** directs promotion at the channel. The manufacturer promotes to wholesalers, wholesalers to retailers, and retailers to consumers. Each member pushes the product to the next.

A **pull strategy** directs promotion at the final customer. Customers then ask retailers for the product, retailers ask wholesalers, and demand pulls the product up the channel.

In health care the choice is constrained. For prescription medicines, the "customer" whom promotion may address is mainly the prescriber. Pull strategies aimed at the public are prohibited in most countries, because patients cannot judge prescription medicines alone. For over-the-counter products and self-paid services, lawful pull strategies are possible.

> **Through Two Lenses**
> - **Medical Laboratory:** A laboratory mostly uses push toward doctors: visits, test menus and scientific evenings explaining new tests. It may use careful pull toward the public for services patients can choose themselves, such as check-up packages. Every message must be accurate and must advise medical consultation where appropriate.
> - **Radiology and Medical Imaging:** An imaging centre mostly uses push toward referring doctors: visits, sample images and fast reporting. It may use careful pull toward the public for booking information and screening awareness campaigns. Every message must state safety facts accurately, such as radiation and contrast risks.

## 9.4 Advertising

**Advertising** is paid, non-personal communication by an identified sponsor, through media, to inform or persuade an audience (Kotler & Keller, 2016). It can be produced in-house, used to build an organisation's standing in the community, or delivered digitally.

### Classifying advertisements

| By reach | By audience | By medium | By function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local | Consumers | Print | To inform |
| Regional | Businesses | Broadcast | To persuade |
| National | Professionals | Online | To remind |
| International | | Outdoor | |

*Table 9.2. Four ways to classify advertisements.*

In health care, advertisements to professionals and to consumers follow different rules. A journal advertisement for a prescription medicine addresses doctors and must carry prescribing information. A newspaper advertisement for a laboratory's opening hours addresses the public and must make no misleading health claims.

### The advertising process

Four parties take part. The **advertiser** pays for the message. The **advertising agency** plans and manages it. The **media** carry it: newspapers, magazines, broadcast, direct mail, posters and, increasingly, websites and social platforms. The **audience** is the target group most likely to need the product, defined by age, location, profession, needs and similar factors.

### What advertising of medicines may do

Advertising of medicines has three legitimate functions. It informs professionals and, where permitted, the public about products and their correct use. It communicates a new, approved indication or formulation. It supports entry into a new market for an approved use. It must stay within the product's approved labelling. Advertising is one tool of the promotion mix, not the best tool; in health care it is the most restricted one.

## 9.5 Sales Promotion

**Sales promotion** offers short-term incentives to buy. Unlike other tools, it can change behaviour quickly. General sales-promotion tools include price discounts, credit terms, free samples, premiums and coupons, contests, special packaging, special campaigns and service offers.

Many of these are restricted in health care. Free samples of prescription medicines may be given only to qualified prescribers, in limited quantities, under industry codes (International Federation of Pharmaceutical Manufacturers & Associations, 2019). Contests and lotteries tied to the purchase of medicines, or to the number of prescriptions, are inappropriate. Coupons for self-paid services must not pressure patients into tests they do not need.

### Advertising and sales promotion compared

| | Advertising | Sales promotion |
|---|---|---|
| Time horizon | Long term | Short term |
| Nature | One-way persuasive message from an identified sponsor | Immediate incentive to buy |
| Cost | Often high | Often lower |
| Link to sales | Indirect | Direct |
| Speed of effect | Slow | Fast |
| Typical aim | Brand building and sales | Sales |

## 9.6 Opinion Leaders and Regulators

An **opinion leader** is a person whose views influence others. In health care, **key opinion leaders** are respected clinicians and scientists whose practice and teaching shape colleagues' choices. Companies may invite them to speak or advise.

Such relationships must be transparent. Payment to a clinician must be for genuine services, at fair value, and must not be linked to prescribing or referrals (International Federation of Pharmaceutical Manufacturers & Associations, 2019).

Regulators and international health bodies are not opinion leaders in this sense. The World Health Organization, the US Food and Drug Administration and the Egyptian Drug Authority are independent public bodies. Their guidance is evidence that communication must respect, not a channel for promotion. Implying that a regulator "recommends" a brand, when it has only approved it, is misleading.

> **In Practice:** A laboratory introducing a new test invites a respected university professor to speak at a scientific evening for doctors. The professor presents published validation data, including the test's limitations. The laboratory pays a fair speaking fee disclosed to the audience, and the invitation carries no link to referrals. The event informs doctors honestly. That is the acceptable form of working with an opinion leader.

## 9.7 The Ethical and Legal Limits of Promotion

The World Health Organization (1988) set out ethical criteria for promoting medicines. The IFPMA Code of Practice governs member companies' promotion to professionals (International Federation of Pharmaceutical Manufacturers & Associations, 2019). National regulators add their own rules. Together they establish principles that apply equally well to laboratories and imaging centres:

1. **Truthfulness.** Claims must be accurate, balanced, supported by evidence and not misleading.
2. **Completeness.** Benefits must be presented with risks and limitations.
3. **Approved use only.** Medicines may be promoted only for their approved indications.
4. **No public promotion of prescription medicines.** Prescription medicines are promoted to professionals, not to the public, in Egypt and most other countries.
5. **No inducements.** Gifts, payments or benefits must not be offered to influence prescribing, dispensing or referral.
6. **Disclosure.** Sponsorship of events, speakers and materials must be disclosed.
7. **Respect for patients.** Communication must not exploit fear, vulnerability or lack of knowledge.

> **Ethics Check:** An imaging centre offers referring doctors a cash payment for every MRI patient they send. This is a referral inducement. It tempts doctors to refer patients who do not need a scan, or to choose the centre for money rather than quality. It breaks professional ethics and, in many countries, the law. The centre may compete for referrals only through quality, speed, reports and fair prices.

> **Common Mistake:** Thinking that small gifts are always harmless. A systematic review of contacts between doctors and the pharmaceutical industry found that such contacts, including small gifts and meals, were associated with changes in prescribing (Fickweiler et al., 2017). Industry codes therefore limit gifts to items of modest value related to practice or education, or ban them entirely, depending on the code.

> **Egyptian Context:** In Egypt, medicine advertising is supervised by the Egyptian Drug Authority, and prescription medicines are promoted to health professionals rather than to the public. Laboratories and imaging centres are bound by professional ethics and by the rules of their licensing bodies. Before running any campaign, check the current rules with the regulator.

## Key Takeaways
- Marketing communications inform, persuade and remind; in health care they must above all be true.
- The main elements are advertising, personal selling, public relations and sales promotion.
- Push promotes through the channel; pull promotes to final customers and is prohibited for prescription medicines to the public.
- Advertisements can be classified by reach, audience, medium and function.
- Sales promotion acts fast but many of its tools are restricted in health care.
- Key opinion leaders are clinicians; regulators are independent bodies, not promotional channels.
- Truthfulness, completeness, approved use, no inducements, disclosure and respect for patients limit all health promotion.

## Answers and Worked Solutions

**Q1. A** — This is the definition of advertising.

**Q2. C** — Exhibitions and conferences are supporting elements of the communication mix.

**Q3. B** — Promotion passed down through channel members is a push strategy.

**Q4. D** — Patients cannot judge prescription medicines unaided, so promotion is directed at prescribers instead.

**Q5. B** — Sales promotion gives an immediate incentive; its effect is fast and tied directly to sales.

**Q6. A** — An advertisement aimed at doctors is professional advertising and must carry prescribing information.

**Q7. C** — Key opinion leaders are influential clinicians and scientists. Regulators are independent public bodies.

**Q8. B** — Approval is not recommendation. Implying endorsement misleads the audience.

**Q9. D** — Paying for referrals is an inducement that corrupts medical judgement.

**Q10. A** — Completeness requires balance: benefits with risks and limitations.

**E1 — model answer points.** Push: promotion through the channel, toward doctors, by visits and scientific evenings; suits a specialised test requiring medical judgement. Pull: promotion to the public; may be used carefully for a self-paid package, with accurate claims and advice to consult a doctor.

**E2 — model answer points.** Time horizon, nature, cost, link to sales, speed, aim. Restricted tools: samples of prescription medicines (prescribers only, limited), contests and lotteries (inappropriate for medicines), coupons that pressure patients into unneeded tests. Restrictions protect patients from inducement and overuse.

**E3 — model answer points.** Truthfulness, completeness, approved use, no public promotion of prescription medicines, no inducements, disclosure, respect for patients. Applied: accurate claims about equipment and reports; honest statements on radiation; no payments to referrers; disclosed sponsorships; no fear-based messages.
