# Glossary

**4 Ps** — Product, price, place and promotion: the four elements of the classic marketing mix (Chapter 4).

**7 Ps of services marketing** — The four Ps plus people, process and physical evidence, used for services such as tests and scans (Chapter 4).

**advertiser** — The person or organisation that pays for an advertisement.

**Advertising** — Paid, non-personal communication by an identified sponsor through media, to inform, persuade or remind an audience.

**advertising agency** — A firm that plans, creates and manages paid marketing communication for advertisers.

**affordable method** — Setting a promotion budget at what the organisation can spare.

**auction price** — A price set by competitive bidding; it may differ sharply from the usual market price.

**audience** — The target group of an advertisement: the people most likely to need the product.

**average cost-effectiveness ratio (ACER)** — The total cost of one option divided by its total effect; the same as the CER.

**benefit–cost ratio** — Total benefits divided by total costs, both in money. Above 1 is a net gain, 1 is break-even, below 1 is a net loss.

**Break-even pricing** — Pricing that uses the volume at which revenue equals total cost; below it the service loses money.

**bundled pricing** — Selling several products or services together for one price, as in a check-up package.

**catchment area** — The area from which a service draws most of its patients.

**channel level** — The number of intermediaries between producer and consumer in a distribution channel.

**Clinical outcomes** — Medical events that result from disease or treatment, such as laboratory values, lives saved or disability days avoided.

**competitive-parity method** — Setting a promotion budget to match competitors' spending.

**Consequences** — The effects, outputs or outcomes of an intervention in an economic evaluation.

**consignment** — An arrangement in which the owner (consignor) keeps ownership and risk of goods held and sold by another (consignee) for a commission.

**Controlled prices** — Prices with a ceiling set by the state, as for registered medicines.

**Convenience products** — Products bought often and quickly with little comparison, such as simple painkillers.

**Cost** — In economic evaluation, the value of the resources an intervention uses.

**Cost-benefit analysis** — An economic evaluation that values both costs and benefits in money, giving a benefit–cost ratio or net benefit.

**Cost-effectiveness analysis** — An economic evaluation that compares options with the same kind of outcome, measured in natural units such as cases cured.

**cost-effectiveness plane** — A graph with the difference in effect on one axis and the difference in cost on the other; its quadrant shows dominance or a trade-off.

**cost-effectiveness ratio (CER)** — The cost of one option divided by its effect.

**cost-effectiveness threshold** — The most a decision-maker is willing to pay for one unit of effect, usually one QALY.

**Cost-minimisation analysis** — An economic evaluation that compares only costs, used when outcomes have been shown to be equivalent.

**Cost-of-illness analysis** — A descriptive study of the total economic burden of a disease. It compares no interventions, so it is not a full economic evaluation, but it gives a baseline.

**Cost-plus pricing** — Setting a price by adding a percentage markup to the full cost per unit.

**Cost-utility analysis** — An economic evaluation that measures outcomes in quality-adjusted life years.

**cost-utility ratio** — Cost per QALY gained; the lower, the better.

**Customer value** — Perceived customer benefit minus total customer cost, where cost includes money, time, effort and anxiety.

**decider** — The person who chooses a product or service; in health care often a professional.

**decline stage** — The stage of the product life cycle marked by a long-run fall in sales.

**Deletion** — Dropping a declining product from the line; the most drastic decline strategy.

**demand** — A want backed by the ability and willingness to pay.

**digital channel** — Any online route by which an organisation reaches its audience, such as search, websites, social media, email or apps.

**Digital marketing** — Promotion of brands and services through the internet and other digital communication; also called online marketing.

**Direct medical costs** — Resources used to prevent, diagnose or treat disease: medicines, tests, professional time, hospital stay and supplies.

**Direct non-medical costs** — Costs that result from illness but are not medical services, such as transport, lodging and special food.

**Discount pricing** — Charging less than competitors to attract price-sensitive customers.

**discount rate** — The yearly rate used in discounting future costs and outcomes.

**Discounting** — Converting future costs and outcomes to their value today, so options with different timings can be compared.

**Distribution** — Planning, organising and controlling the movement of products from where they are made to where they are used.

**distribution channel** — The set of firms or individuals that take ownership of a product, or help transfer it, from producer to consumer.

**Durable goods** — Goods that survive many uses over a long time, usually three years or more.

**Earned media** — Coverage an organisation does not pay for or control, such as reviews, shares and news.

**ECHO model** — The grouping of outcomes into economic, clinical and humanistic kinds.

**Economic outcomes** — Changes in resource use and money that result from an intervention, such as savings or production regained.

**elastic** — Describes demand whose percentage change exceeds the percentage change in price (absolute elasticity above 1).

**exchange** — Obtaining something of value by offering something in return.

**fail points** — The steps of a service where it most often goes wrong, such as sample labelling or safety screening.

**Fixed costs** — Costs that do not change with volume in the short run, such as rent and equipment leases.

**Fixed prices** — Prices set by a state body that the seller cannot change.

**foregone earnings approach** — Another name for the human capital approach to valuing lost productivity.

**Free prices** — Prices set by agreement between buyer and seller through supply and demand, without state intervention.

**friction period** — The time until a sick worker is replaced or output is otherwise restored; the friction cost approach values lost production only over this period.

**Functional value** — What an offering does for the customer: the solution it provides.

**Going-rate pricing** — Charging about what competitors charge.

**good** — A physical product made for sale to satisfy a need.

**growth stage** — The stage of the product life cycle in which sales rise quickly and competitors enter.

**Harvesting** — Keeping a declining product while cutting its marketing support.

**Health economics** — The study of how scarce resources are allocated to produce health and health care.

**health-related quality of life (HRQOL)** — The effect of health and treatment on a person's physical, social and emotional well-being.

**healthcare market** — The set of patients, professionals, organisations and payers who exchange health goods and services.

**heuristic** — A mental shortcut used in choosing, such as buying what the family has always used.

**hub-and-spoke** — A network with one central facility and many small collection or service points.

**Humanistic outcomes** — The effects of disease or treatment on a patient's function, well-being and quality of life.

**Incremental cost** — The additional cost of one option compared with another.

**incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER)** — The difference in cost between two options divided by the difference in their effects.

**Indirect costs** — The value of lost productivity: morbidity cost plus mortality cost.

**inelastic** — Describes demand whose percentage change is smaller than the percentage change in price (absolute elasticity below 1).

**influencer** — A person who shapes a choice without making it, such as a relative or pharmacist.

**information asymmetry** — A gap in knowledge between provider and customer; in health care the patient usually knows less.

**inputs** — The resources an intervention uses, measured as costs.

**Intangible costs** — Non-financial burdens of disease and care, such as pain, suffering and anxiety.

**intermediaries** — Channel members between the producer and the final customer.

**introduction stage** — The stage of the product life cycle after launch, when acceptance is uncertain and sales grow slowly.

**key opinion leaders** — Respected clinicians and scientists whose views shape colleagues' practice.

**law of demand** — Other things equal, the quantity demanded falls as the price rises.

**line of visibility** — In a service blueprint, the line separating the staff actions the customer sees from those behind the scenes.

**market** — The set of actual and potential buyers of a product.

**market segmentation** — Dividing a market into groups of buyers who respond differently to marketing, including price.

**Marketing** — The activity, institutions and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings of value for customers and society.

**Marketing communications** — The means by which an organisation informs, persuades and reminds its market.

**marketing mix** — The set of controllable tools an organisation uses to reach its market.

**marketing orientation** — An approach that starts from customers' needs and builds the offering around them.

**markup** — An amount added to cost, expressed as a percentage of cost; a margin is the same amount expressed as a percentage of the price.

**maturity stage** — The stage of the product life cycle in which sales growth slows, competition peaks and prices fall.

**media** — The channels that carry messages, such as print, broadcast, outdoor and online media.

**medical device** — An instrument, apparatus, material or other article for diagnosis, prevention, monitoring or treatment that does not act mainly by pharmacological means.

**medicine** — A substance in a dosage form intended to treat, prevent or diagnose disease, or to modify physiological functions.

**Micro-costing** — Building a total cost from the units of each resource used times their unit costs.

**Monetary value** — The price paid relative to the perceived worth of an offering.

**morbidity cost** — Earnings or production lost because of illness.

**mortality cost** — Production lost because of premature death.

**need** — A state of felt deprivation, such as the need for a diagnosis.

**net benefit** — Total benefits minus total costs, in money.

**Non-durable goods** — Goods used once or over a short time, such as reagents and most medicines.

**objective-and-task method** — Setting a promotion budget by costing the tasks needed to reach the campaign's objective.

**opinion leader** — A person whose views influence others.

**Opportunity cost** — The value of the best alternative use of the resources actually used.

**outcomes** — The health and economic consequences of an intervention.

**outcomes research** — Research that measures economic, clinical and humanistic outcomes.

**Owned media** — Channels an organisation controls, such as its own website, app and accounts.

**Paid media** — Advertising space an organisation buys, such as search or social-media advertisements.

**payer** — The party that funds a product or service: patient, insurer, employer or state.

**Penetration** — A pricing strategy that sets a low entry price to gain share quickly.

**People** — In the services mix, everyone who meets the customer during the service.

**percentage-of-sales method** — Setting a promotion budget as a fixed share of revenue.

**Personal selling** — Face-to-face presentation to buyers, such as a representative's visit to a doctor.

**Perspective** — Whose costs and consequences an economic evaluation counts: patient, provider, payer or society.

**Pharmaceutical marketing** — The part of health-care marketing concerned with medicines, directed at professionals and, within limits, consumers.

**pharmaceutical warehouse** — A licensed establishment that stores medicines and supplies them wholesale.

**pharmacoeconomic evaluation** — Any study that assesses the costs and consequences of alternative therapies.

**Pharmacoeconomics** — The branch of health economics that analyses the costs and consequences of medicines and drug therapy.

**Physical evidence** — In the services mix, the tangible signs of an intangible service: setting, equipment, reports and certificates.

**Place** — The element of the mix concerned with how a product reaches its customers.

**Premium pricing** — Charging more than competitors, justified by better service or a stronger brand.

**present value** — The value today of a future cost or outcome: F ÷ (1 + r) to the power t.

**Price** — The amount charged for a product, or the total of what customers give up to obtain it.

**Price elasticity of demand** — The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

**price policy** — An organisation's set of rules for determining and managing prices.

**primary demand** — Demand for a product category rather than for one brand.

**Process** — In the services mix, the steps a customer goes through to receive the service.

**product** — Anything offered to satisfy a need or want, whether a good or a service.

**product life cycle (PLC)** — The pattern of sales and profit of a product over time: introduction, growth, maturity and decline.

**product orientation** — An approach that assumes customers want the highest quality, sometimes beyond what they value.

**production orientation** — An approach that assumes customers want what is cheap and available, and focuses on efficiency.

**Promotion** — The element of the mix concerned with communication: advertising, public relations, personal selling and sales promotion.

**Psychological value** — How far a product helps customers feel better or express themselves.

**Public relations** — Building goodwill and reputation through news, community activity and information.

**pull strategy** — Promotion aimed at final customers, whose demand pulls the product up the channel.

**push strategy** — Promotion aimed at channel members, each of whom pushes the product to the next.

**Quality** — In health care, technical quality (a correct result) and functional quality (how the service is delivered).

**quality-adjusted life year (QALY)** — A measure combining length and quality of life: years of life times utility.

**relationship** — A long-term, trusting link between an organisation and its customer.

**Retail prices** — Prices at which goods are sold to the public.

**Retail trade** — Selling goods and services to final consumers for personal use.

**Sales promotion** — Short-term incentives to encourage purchase, such as samples, coupons and discounts.

**Satisfaction** — The customer's judgement of whether a service met expectations.

**Search engine optimisation (SEO)** — Making a website easier for search engines to find and rank, mainly through clear and useful content.

**selective demand** — Demand for one brand rather than for the product category.

**selling orientation** — An approach that assumes customers will not buy enough unless pushed.

**Sensitivity analysis** — Recalculating a result with different plausible values to see whether the conclusion changes.

**service** — An act or performance offered to another; it tends to be intangible, its capacity cannot be stored, and the customer usually takes part in producing it.

**service blueprint** — A diagram of every step a customer takes through a service, with the staff actions and support processes behind each step.

**Shopping products** — Products compared on quality, price and suitability before purchase.

**Skimming** — A pricing strategy that sets a high entry price and lowers it over time.

**Social value** — How far using a product connects the customer with others.

**societal marketing orientation** — A marketing orientation that also protects the long-term interest of society; the only acceptable one for health services.

**Specialty products** — Products with unique features for which buyers make a special effort.

**stock exchange quotation** — The price of goods or securities traded on an exchange.

**supplier-induced demand** — Demand created when a provider who advises also sells, and recommends more than is needed.

**Tariffs** — Prices for services, such as a schedule of fees for tests or scans.

**teleradiology** — Sending images electronically to radiologists elsewhere for reporting.

**tenders** — Sealed competitive bids by suppliers to provide goods or services to a stated specification.

**Through Two Lenses** — The box in every chapter that applies the chapter's idea to the medical laboratory and to radiology and medical imaging.

**Traditional marketing** — Marketing through print, broadcast, outdoor signs, direct mail and personal visits.

**unique selling proposition (USP)** — A true and relevant benefit that competitors do not offer.

**Unsought products** — Products buyers do not know about or do not think of buying, such as many screening tests.

**user** — The person who receives or uses a product or service; in health care, the patient.

**utility** — A number valuing a health state, where 1 is full health and 0 is death; some instruments allow values below 0 for states judged worse than death.

**Value-based pricing** — Setting a price from the value a product creates for the customer.

**Variable costs** — Costs that rise with each unit produced, such as reagents and disposables.

**want** — The form a need takes, shaped by culture and experience.

**Wholesale prices** — Prices at which producers sell to firms and organisations.

**world market price** — The price recognised in international trade for a commodity.
