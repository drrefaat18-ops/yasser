| C-001 | major | Chapter 2, §2.4, “Renal dysfunction, cirrhosis, congestive heart failure and oedema all raise \(V_d\)”; MCQ 8 | The direction of a disease-related change in apparent volume of distribution is not universal. It depends on drug lipophilicity, protein binding, tissue perfusion and whether extracellular fluid is expanded. MCQ 8 does not specify the drug but treats oedema as necessarily increasing \(V_d\). | The statement teaches an unsafe rule and leaves the MCQ with no uniquely defensible answer for an unspecified drug. | Replace “all raise” with a drug-dependent explanation. In MCQ 8, specify a predominantly extracellular, hydrophilic drug with limited protein binding. |
| C-002 | minor | Chapter 3, §3.6, “Two-way ANOVA is appropriate; Student t-test is not, because it ignores subject…” | Neither conclusion follows without specifying the design and comparison. A paired t-test does account for within-subject pairing, while crossover or repeated observations commonly require a crossover ANOVA or mixed-effects model with explicitly defined effects. | Students may incorrectly believe all t-tests ignore subjects or that two-way ANOVA is universally appropriate. | State the study design and endpoint, then specify the corresponding paired, crossover-ANOVA or mixed-effects analysis. |
| C-003 | major | Chapter 5, §5.3, “After five half-lives … when a monitoring sample should be drawn”; “trough sample”; MCQ 3 | A constant infusion has no dose-cycle peak or trough, so a sample during uninterrupted infusion is a steady-state concentration, not a trough. Five half-lives is also not a universal earliest sampling rule: properly timed pre-steady-state concentrations can be clinically interpretable. | This can produce incorrect therapeutic-drug-monitoring terminology and delayed or inappropriate sampling. | Call it a steady-state sample and state that sampling time is drug-, indication- and model-specific; pre-steady-state samples may be used when their timing is incorporated into interpretation. |
| C-004 | minor | Chapter 6, §6.6, “Clearance ratio zero: Filtered, then completely reabsorbed, as for glucose.” | Glucose clearance approaches zero only while filtered load remains below the renal transport maximum. Once the threshold is exceeded, glucose appears in urine and its clearance is nonzero. | The unqualified example hides a core feature of saturable tubular transport. | Add “at plasma concentrations below the renal threshold/transport maximum.” |
| C-005 | minor | Chapter 7, §7.7, “If \(K\) is greater than \(0.69\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}\), flip-flop kinetics has a good chance of occurring.” | There is no universal elimination-rate threshold for flip-flop kinetics. It occurs when absorption is slower than elimination, meaning \(K_a<K\), regardless of whether \(K\) exceeds \(0.69\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}\). | The numerical cutoff can lead students to diagnose flip-flop kinetics without estimating or comparing \(K_a\). | Remove the cutoff and require comparison of independently supported absorption and elimination rate constants. |
| C-006 | major | Chapter 8, Key Takeaways, “Time to steady state depends on the half-life only”; compare §8.4 and Equation 8.2 | The takeaway contradicts the chapter’s oral multiple-dose equation and accompanying text: the absorption term containing \(K_a\) can materially affect approach to the plateau, particularly when absorption is slow or flip-flop kinetics occurs. | A prominently stated summary replaces the chapter’s qualified result with an incorrect universal rule. | Restrict the half-life-only statement to constant infusion and repeated IV boluses under linear kinetics. State separately that oral accumulation can also depend on \(K_a\). |
| C-007 | minor | Glossary, “Noncompartmental analysis (NCA) — … requires only linear kinetics”; compare Chapter 9, §9.1 | NCA can compute AUC, AUMC and MRT from nonlinear data. Linear kinetics is needed for interpreting clearance and volume estimates as dose-independent parameters, not for performing NCA itself. Chapter 9 correctly makes this distinction. | The glossary contradicts the chapter and gives an incorrect definition of a central method. | Define NCA as model-independent numerical moment analysis, then qualify which derived parameters require linear, time-invariant kinetics. |
| C-008 | minor | Chapter 11, §11.1, “The polar metabolite distributes less widely and its \(V_d\) is smaller.” | Greater polarity often reduces distribution, but it does not guarantee a smaller apparent \(V_d\). Binding, transporters, ion trapping and tissue affinity can reverse the expected relationship. | An empirical tendency is presented as a pharmacokinetic identity. | Change to “often distributes less widely and may have a smaller \(V_d\), depending on binding and transport.” |
| C-009 | minor | Chapter 12, §12.2, “propranolol reaches the liver faster and saturates more of its first-pass metabolism” | The asserted saturation mechanism is not established by the cited phenomenon. Human studies instead describe reduced hepatic extraction associated with increased hepatic entry or blood flow and acknowledge that the mechanism is not fully explained. [PubMed 3046813](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3046813/); [PubMed 2379531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2379531/). | It converts a proposed, multifactorial explanation into a definite enzyme-saturation mechanism. | State that protein-rich meals may increase propranolol bioavailability by reducing first-pass hepatic extraction; describe the mechanism as incompletely resolved. |
| C-010 | minor | Chapter 13, §13.1, “The concept dates from 1945, when Oser and colleagues…” | This specific historical attribution remains unsupported. The text itself says the supplied source notes name the study without citing it, yet retains the claim without adding a primary or reliable secondary reference. This is a surviving part of first-edition finding F-031, despite the rework’s claim to have repaired unsupported named-study history. | Readers still cannot verify a precise historical claim that the prior audit expressly identified as problematic. | Add a verifiable bibliographic citation supporting the 1945 attribution or remove the name and date. |
| C-011 | major | Chapter 13, §13.3, “\(C_{p\max}\) and \(t_{\max}\) measure the rate of absorption”; Key Takeaways; compare Chapter 1 MCQ 2 rationale and Glossary entry for \(t_{\max}\) | \(C_{\max}\) depends on both absorption and extent of exposure, as well as disposition; \(t_{\max}\) is sensitive to absorption rate but is not a direct or unique measure of \(K_a\). Chapter 1 correctly says \(C_{\max}\) measures neither rate nor extent alone, so the book gives incompatible definitions. | The contradiction affects interpretation of bioequivalence endpoints and can make assessment answers depend on which chapter the student follows. | Consistently describe \(C_{\max}\) and \(t_{\max}\) as rate-sensitive endpoints, with \(C_{\max}\) also influenced by extent and disposition and \(t_{\max}\) not uniquely determining \(K_a\). |
| C-012 | major | Chapter 13, §13.5, “The risk that a patient receives a product that is truly outside the limits is held at 5%”; MCQ 7 rationale, “The patient’s risk is still held at 5%.” | The 5% level belongs to the study’s statistical test: TOST evaluates each one-sided hypothesis at \(\alpha=0.05\), corresponding to a 90% confidence interval. It is not a 5% patient-level probability of receiving a nonequivalent product. [ICH M13A](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_M13A_Step4_Final_Guideline_2024_0723.pdf). | This is a fundamental misinterpretation of bioequivalence inference and makes the keyed MCQ rationale false even though the selected option is otherwise defensible. | Replace “patient’s risk” with “study-level Type I error of incorrectly concluding equivalence at the null boundary.” Rewrite the MCQ rationale accordingly. |
| C-013 | major | Chapter 13, §13.6, “aqueous solubility below 5 mg/mL” and “slow dissolution below 50% in 30 min” | These are presented as current physicochemical warning thresholds, but they do not match the current ICH/EDA BCS framework. Current criteria use dose solubility in no more than 250 mL across the specified pH range and rapid dissolution of at least 85% within the applicable time, rather than universal 5 mg/mL and 50%-in-30-min cutoffs. [EDA Bioequivalence Guideline, Version 4/2026](https://www.edaegypt.gov.eg/media/cw1hkuea/egyptian-guideline-for-conducting-bioequivale_vr-4-2026.pdf). | Students could apply obsolete or invented thresholds when assessing biowaiver eligibility and product risk. | Replace the two thresholds with the current dose/solubility-volume and 85% dissolution criteria, including pH range, apparatus and time conditions. |
| C-014 | minor | Chapter 14, §14.2, “Inside the body, [sink conditions are] maintained naturally because absorption removes dissolved drug.” | Gastrointestinal sink conditions are not guaranteed. Poor permeability, high dose, limited luminal volume, supersaturation or precipitation can allow dissolved concentration to approach saturation. | The absolute claim overstates how directly in-vitro dissolution assumptions translate in vivo. | State that absorption can help maintain an in-vivo concentration gradient, but physiological sink conditions are drug-, dose- and formulation-dependent. |
