Pharmacokinetics
Kinetic study (study of the rate process) of ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism and
elimination)
Biopharmaceutics
Science that study relationship between the physicochemical properties of the drug (active
pharmaceutical ingredient API), dosage form and route of administration on the rate and
extent of drug absorption
Biopharmaceutics study factors that affect
1) Stability of drug in the dosage form product
2) Drug release from the product
3) Rate of drug release from the product
4) Systemic absorption of drug
Bioavailability = physiological availability = biological availability:
the rate and extent of drug absorption from dosage form and become available at the site of
action. For drug products intended not to reach blood; their rate and extent will be measured
by assessing effect of drug at site of action e.g. antacids.

Pharmacokinetic models
 A mathematical model (equation) simulating the rate process of ADME and predict
concentration of drug in the body at any time.
Applications of pharmacokinetic models
1. Used to estimate possible accumulation of drug inside the body
2. Allow maintaining drug concentration within therapeutic range (TI) thus optimize the
therapy
3. Used to correlate concentration of drug with therapeutic response
 Simple models are compartmentally based models where group of organs or tissues are
grouped to one or more compartments where drug move to or from central compartments
(blood)
 They are divided based on number of compartments into
1) One compartment open model
Central compartment (1) K Elimination
One compartment open model following IV injection
Absorption Ka Central compartment (1) K Elimination
One compartment open model following first order
Assumptions
oral absorption
a) Body is one central compartment (plasma and highly perfused organs) to which drug is
added and eliminated
b) Once drug is injected into blood; it undergo instantaneous equilibrium and distribution into
the surrounding tissues
c) Concentration of drug is proportional to dose (linear relationship)
d) Elimination of drug is first order (rate of drug elimination depend on concentration of drug
in blood)
e) Changes in plasma level of the drug will result in proportional changes in tissue and urine
drug levels

Clearance
 The volume of the fluid (plasma or blood) containing the drug which is cleared of the drug
per unit time, so if clearance of penicillin is 15 ml / min in a patient with Vd = 12 liters,
this means that 15 ml of the 12 liters is cleared of the drug per minute. Its unit is volume
per time (ml/min or L/hr)
 It is a measure of drug elimination from the body without identifying the mechanism or the
process (may be through kidney excretion, liver metabolism or both)
 It is a constant for a first order process regardless of drug concentration

Validity of urine data
1- At least 20 % of the drug should be excreted unchanged (as intact drug).
2- Collection period: Urine samples are collected till most of the drug is excreted i.e. to
about 7 t½ where 99 % of drug is eliminated or 10 t ½ where 99.9% of drug is eliminated.
3- Sampling interval: Frequent sampling to get a good curve description.
4- Subjects should be instructed for complete evacuation of the bladder at each collection
period to collect the entire drug present in bladder.
5- Considering alteration in urinary PH and volume which may cause variation in urinary
excretion rate.
6- Assay technique should be accurate and specific for the drug (HPLC).
7- Statistical analysis should be done to compensate the individual variation (2 way ANOVA
is used not student ''t'' test)

 Compartmental models should always use the fewest number of compartments necessary
to describe the experimental data adequately.
 The observed number of compartments will depend on
1) The route of drug administration
2) The rate of drug absorption
3) The total time of sampling
4) The number of samples taken during the collection period
5) The assay sensitivity

Drug elimination from the body
It is irreversible removal of drug from the body by all routes of elimination
 Routes of drug elimination
1) Excretion: mainly renal excretion (kidney) or excretion into sweat, bile, saliva or milk
2) Metabolism (biotransformation): enzymatic conversion of drug into metabolite
in liver
Renal excretion of drug
 It is the route of excretion for non volatile, water soluble, low molecular weight and slowly
metabolized drugs
 Drugs are removed in kidney through one or combination of the following
1) Glomerular filteration
2) Active tubular secretion
3) Renal tubular reabsorption

Zero order absorption
 Drug is absorbed from GI lumen to blood by a constant rate but elimination is first order
 e.g. Osmotic pump, sustained release tablets
 Similar to administration of drug by IV infusion

First order one compartment absorption model
 Absorption is first order for rapidly dissolving dosage forms e.g. immediate release tablets,
capsules, suppositories and IM aqueous injections
 Elimination is first order
 Drug undergo instantaneous equilibrium and distribution (no distribution phase)

The pH -partition hypothesis of drug absorption
The pH-partition theory states that drugs are absorbed from the biological
membranes by passive diffusion, depending on the fraction of un-ionized
form of the drug at the pH of the fluids close to that biological membrane.
 In solution, organic electrolytes exist in non ionized (lipid-soluble) and ionized (poor lipid
soluble) form. The fraction of each species depends on the pH of the solution.
 Since the GI barriers are much more permeable to unchanged, lipid-soluble solutes, a drug
may be well-absorbed from one segment of the GIT, where a favorable pH exists but
poorly absorbed from another segment, where a less favorable pH is found.
 The gastrointestinal tracts acts as a lipophilic barrier and thus ionized drugs, compared to
un-ionized ones, are more hydrophilic and have minimal membrane transport.
 The absorption of weakly basic drugs such as antihistamines and antidepressants is
favored in the small intestine where such drugs exist largely in a non ionized form.
 On the other hand, the acidic gastric fluids tend to retard the absorption of weak bases but
promote the absorption of weakly acidic drugs such as sulphonamides and NSAIDs.
 Changes in the pH of the fluids in a given segment of tract may improve or impede the
absorption of a drug. The pH of gastric fluid varies considerably.
 Gastric secretions have a pH of less than 1, but the pH of gastric contents is usually
between 1 and 3 because of dilution and diet. The pH of the stomach contents is distinctly
but briefly elevated after meals; pH values of 5 are not unusual.
 Fasting tends to decrease the pH of gastric fluids.
 Disease may also influence the pH of the stomach. The average gastric pH is significantly
lower in patients with a duodenal ulcer than in healthy individuals.
 Fats and fatty acids in the diet have been found to inhibit gastric secretions.
 A major clinical effect of antispasmodic drugs such as Atropine and H -blockers, like
2
cimetidine and ranitidine is a reduction in gastric secretions.
 Some anticholinergic activity, including suppression of gastric secretion, is commonly
found with many other drugs. Antacids are widely used for the purpose of neutralizing
gastric acidity and elevating the pH of gastric contents.

Limitations of pH-partition hypothesis theory
1) Most weak acids are well-absorbed from the small intestine, which is contrary to the
prediction of the pH-partition hypothesis.
2) Quaternary ammonium compounds are ionized at all pHs, but are readily absorbed
from the GI tract.
3) Barbitone (pKa 7.8) which is almost totally unionized at gastric pH is only poorly
absorbed from the stomach; however thiopentone, which has a similar pKa value (pKa 7.6)
is much better absorbed from the stomach than barbitone because the unionized form
of thiopentone is more lipid soluble than the unionized form of barbitone,
exhibits a greater affinity for the gastrointestinal membrane and thus better absorbed than
barbitone from stomach
These discrepancies arise because pH-partition theory does not take into
consideration the following (this deviation can be explained by)
1) Large epithelial surface areas of small intestine compensates for ionization effects.
2) Long residence time in the small intestine also compensates for ionization effects.
3) Charged drugs, such as quaternary ammonium compounds and tetracyclines, may interact
with opposite charged organic ions, resulting in a neutral species, which is absorbable (ion
pair mechanism)
4) Some drugs are absorbed via active transport

 The reduction in the particle size of Griseofulvin, Chloramphenicol, and Tetracycline

Reduction in particle size is not always helpful, rationalize?
The reduction in particle size of drugs e.g. Aspirin, Phenacetin, Phenobarbital, by

The decrease in surface area is attributed to the following factors:
a) On their surface, hydrophobic drugs absorb air and thereby reduce their wettability.
b) The particles having size below 0.1 micron reaggregate to form larger particles.
c) The particles having certain microsize get electrical charge, thereby preventing contact
with wetting medium.

Design and evaluation of bioequivalence study
 The bioavailability of the generic drug product (new test product) is compared to the

 Statistical analysis is used to detect difference in bioavailability that are not due to

 The main reason for bioequivalence study is that in the past products that were
thought to be pharmaceutical equivalents did not give similar therapeutic effects in

1) Design
The basic design of bioequivalence study is determined by
1- The scientific question to be answered (to prove bioequivalence or bio-
nonequivalence or to compare rate and extent or rate only)
2- The nature of the reference material and the dosage form to be tested
3- The availability and sensitivity of the analytical methods
4- Benefit-risk and ethical considerations with regard to testing in humans
 Both reference and test products should have the same dose, dosage form and route
of administration
 Bioequivalence study may be done as single dose or multiple dose study (steady state)
or both
 Bioequivalence study is done on normal, healthy male and female volunteers who
have signed consent to be in the study (consent required only in human studies)

Alternative Methods of Dissolution Testing
Rotating Bottle Method
 The rotating bottle method was used mainly for controlled-release beads.
 The dissolution medium may be easily changed, such as from artificial gastric juice to
artificial intestinal juice.
 The equipment consists of a rotating rack that holds the sample drug products in bottles.
The bottles are capped tightly and rotated in a 37°C temperature bath.
 At various times, the samples are removed from the bottle, decanted through a 40-mesh
screen, and the residues are assayed. To the remaining drug residues within the bottles
are added an equal volume of fresh medium and the dissolution test is continued.
 A dissolution test with pH 1.2 medium for 1 hour, pH 2.5 medium for the next 1 hour,
followed by pH 4.5 medium for 1.5 hours, pH 7 medium for 1.5 hours, and pH 7.5
medium for 2 hours was recommended to simulate condition of the gastrointestinal tract.
 Disadvantage is that this procedure is manual and tedious.
Intrinsic dissolution Method
 Used for screening of a new drug or substance without the effect of excipients
 The dissolution of a drug powder by maintaining a constant surface is called intrinsic
dissolution.
 Intrinsic dissolution is usually expressed as mg/cm2/min.
Diffusion Cells
Franz diffusion cell
 The Franz diffusion cell is a static diffusion system that is used for characterizing drug
permeation through a skin model.
 The source of skin may be human cadaver ةثجskin or animal skin (hairless mouse skin).
 The skin is mounted on the Franz diffusion cell system.
 The drug permeates across the skin into a receptor fluid compartment that may be
sampled at various times.
 The Franz diffusion cell system is useful for comparing in-vitro drug release profiles and
skin permeation characteristics to aid in selecting an appropriate formulation that has
optimum drug delivery.

Problems of variable control in dissolution testing
Variations of 25% or more may occur with the same type of equipment and procedure.
Variations maybe due to
a) Central alignment of the paddle in the paddle method
b) Turbulence can create increased agitation, resulting in a higher dissolution rate
c) The basket method is more sensitive to clogging due to gummy materials. Pieces of small
particles can also clog up the basket screen and create a local non-sink condition for
dissolution
d) Dissolved gas in the mediwn may form air bubbles on the surface of the dosage form unit
and can affect dissolution in both the basket and paddle methods.
 A low-density preparation may be poorly wetted in the basket method.
 A floating dosage form (e.g., suppository) may be placed in a stainless steel coil (sinker) so
that the dosage forms at the bottom of the dissolution flask.
In-vitro - In-vivo correlation (IVIVC)
 A relationship between a biological property of the drug (such as phannacodynamic
effect, plasma drug concentration or rate of absorption) and a physicochemical property of
the drug product containing the drug substance, such as dissolution rate
 In order to have an IVIVC, some property of the drug release from the drug product in
vitro under specified conditions, must relate to in-vivo drug performance.
 An IVIVC should be evaluated to demonstrate that predictability of in-vivo performance
of a drug product from its in-vitro dissolution characteristics is maintained over a range of
in-vitro dissolution release rates and manufacturing changes.
 The in-vitro dissolution characteristics are dependent on the physical properties of the
active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), the drug formulation, the hydrodynamics of the
dissolution apparatus, and dissolution medium.
