## 1.1 Sore Throat

Definition: Pain in any part of throat mucosa due to common cold (pharynx → pharyngitis; tonsils → tonsillitis).

Distinction: Between pharyngitis and tonsillitis is unclear.

Age: <3 yrs → strep rare. 5–10 yrs + 15–25 yrs → strep common; Children →; Adolescents → glandular fever; Older age → carcinoma risk.

Figure labels (page 7): White drainage patch; Swollen and sore throat; Tonsil.

Cause: Viral 70–90% of cases: 1 cough; 2 pharyngeal inflammation ulcer ± exudate; 3 fever, headache.

Cause: Bacterial ~10–30% (most common: Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus / S. pyogenes): 1 absence of cough; 2 Tonsillar exudate; 3 fever, tender cervical lymph nodes.

Complications of Streptococcal Throat: Otitis media; Acute sinusitis.

## 1.2 Sore Throat: OTC Treatment

Local anesthetics → Benzocaine, Lidocaine.

Antibacterials/Antiviral → Penicillin or macrolides (if allergy).

Anti-inflammatories → Benzydamine spray.

Analgesics → Flurbiprofen, Paracetamol, ibuprofen effective.

Aspirin/saltwater gargles.

Age (page 11): Benzydamine spray, Rinse, Lidocaine, Flurbiprofen → >12 years. Benzocaine → Lozenge >3 yrs; Spray >6 yrs. Benzydamine spray → Spray/lozenge >6 yrs. [The page lists Benzydamine spray in both the >12 years group and the >6 yrs row; to be confirmed with the course doctor.]

Pregnancy (page 12): Flurbiprofen → avoid in 3trimester) pregnancy. Lidocaine → Not safe. Benzydamine spray, Rinse → Not safe.

Breast feeding → Flurbiprofen ✓.

## 1.3 Tonsillitis

Definition: Inflammation of the palatine tonsils due to infection (viral or bacterial).

Age: Children (5–15 years).

Features: sore throat, fever, and tonsillar enlargement ± exudate.

## 1.4 Tonsillitis: Cause and Pathology

Cause: Viral (70–80%): Rhinovirus, adenovirus, coronavirus, influenza.

Cause: Bacterial 20–30%: Group A β-hemolytic streptococcus (S. pyogenes) – most important; also Groups C & G strep, Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae. Key pathogen for complications.

Pathology: Pathogenesis Infection → crypt inflammation → erythema, edema, exudates.

Immune response: Local + systemic inflammatory reaction → fever, lymphadenopathy.

## 1.5 Viral versus Bacterial Tonsillitis

ONSET: Viral More gradual; Bacterial Sudden.

Throat pain: Viral Mild to moderate; Bacterial severe.

fever: Viral low; Bacterial high>38.

Cough, coryza, rhinorrhea: Viral Common; Bacterial absent.

Tonsils: Viral Red, swollen, little/no exudate; Bacterial Enlarged, purulent exudates.

Lymph nodes: Viral Mild or absent; Bacterial Tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy.

Duration: Viral 5–7 days, self-limiting; Bacterial 7–10 days, needs antibiotics if strep confirmed.

Systemic: Viral Malaise, fatigue, conjunctivitis; Bacterial Headache, abdominal pain (children).

## 1.6 Tonsillitis: Complications

Suppurative (local): 1 severe sore throat; 2 Retropharyngeal abscess Deep neck space infection, airway compromise; 3 Otitis media Spread via Eustachian tube; 4 Sinusitis Due to obstruction of sinus drainage.

Non-suppurative (systemic, post-streptococcal): 1 Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF); 2 Scarlet fever strawberry tongue, sandpaper rash; 3 Post-streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN): Immune complex deposition in kidneys; Hematuria, edema, hypertension, proteinuria.

## 1.7 Tonsillitis: Management

1 NonPharmacological: hydration, warm saline gargles • Soft diet ✓; Avoid irritants (smoke, spicy food ✗.

2 Pharmacological: 1 Analgesics/antipyretics → Paracetamol, ibuprofen First-line. 2 Topical anesthetics → Benzydamine spray, lidocaine/benzocaine lozenges Local pain relief. 3 NSAID lozenges → Flurbiprofen Some efficacy. 4 Antibiotics.

3 Tonsillectomy Indications: Obstructive sleep apnea from tonsillar hypertrophy • Recurrent peritonsillar abscess • Suspicion of malignancy. ≥7 episodes in 1 year • ≥5 episodes/year for 2 years • ≥3 episodes/year for 3 years.

## 1.8 Tonsillitis: Antibiotics

First-line: ✓ Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) 10-day course. Children: 12.5–25 mg/kg 2–4 times daily; Adults: 500 mg QDS.

✗ Amoxicillin 500 mg TDS 10 days. Avoid if EBV Glandular fever (EBV) fever, malaise, rash, splenomegaly suspected (rash).

Alternatives (Penicillin Allergy): ✓ Clarithromycin 250–500 mg BD 5 days Better tolerated than erythromycin. ✓ Azithromycin 500 mg OD 3 days Short course. ✗ Erythromycin 250–500 mg QDS 7–10 days GI side effects common.

Product pictures (page 21): Augmentin; Zithromax; Klacid.
