# Design — Entrepreneurship in Healthcare: From a Health Need to a Sustainable Venture

Inputs: `brief.json`, `rubric.json`, `template.json`, `theme.json`, `agents/health-entrepreneurship-reviewer.json`
(DEC-001); `evaluation/report.md`, `evaluation/findings.json`, `evaluation/scorecard.json` (24.5 / 100),
`evaluation/codex-review.md`, `evaluation/fixes.md` (external reviewer per DEC-002).

## 1. What this book is

A first textbook of entrepreneurship for third-year (Level 3) students of the Faculty of Applied Health Sciences, East
Port Said National University. Its readers will work in medical laboratories, imaging centres and other health
services. Some will found one; most will work inside one and will need to think like a founder: see an unmet need,
test an idea with real patients and payers, and build a service that is safe, lawful and able to pay its way.

The source is a set of revision notes for three lectures (entrepreneurship; global organisations; national culture)
with question banks. The book keeps every topic of those lectures, in their order, and applies each one to health
ventures. It then adds the path the notes never teach: from a health need to a launched and sustainable venture.
Chanda and Gupta (2025), *Healthcare Entrepreneurship and Management*, is the structural reference for that path; it is
cited where it is used, and none of its text, figures or data tables is reproduced. The text is new; the notes supply
the syllabus, not the wording.

## 2. Principles

1. **Health ventures first.** Every concept is taught for a health venture: a laboratory, an imaging centre, a
   pharmacy, a clinic, a digital health service or a device. Foreign technology cases (Apple, Alibaba, Tesla) are kept
   briefly where the source uses them, and each is set beside a health example.
2. **Real Egyptian entrepreneurs, verified.** Every chapter has an Egyptian Entrepreneur box: a founder or venture
   from the Egyptian health sector, chosen because their story teaches that chapter's idea. Every fact in a box (names,
   years, services, funding amounts, size) is taken from a cited public source (company history page, regulator,
   development-finance report, reputable press) and dated "as reported in YEAR". A fact that cannot be checked is left
   out. The boxes describe what the venture did; they never advertise it, rank it against competitors or judge its
   prices or quality. Section 6 lists the planned profiles and their starting sources.
3. **Patients before growth.** Every chapter carries an Ethics Check. Patient safety, privacy, informed consent,
   honest communication and fair access are treated as conditions of a health venture, not as costs. Chapter 12 treats
   regulation, quality and ethics in full, and Chapter 14 teaches social entrepreneurship as a form of the same skills.
4. **Two lenses.** Every chapter has a Through Two Lenses box with one paragraph for the Medical Laboratory and one for
   Radiology and Medical Imaging, balanced in length (template tolerance 20%).
5. **Tools that are used, numbers that are checked.** Need statements, personas, empathy maps, the Business Model
   Canvas, competitor grids, PESTEL and Porter's five forces are built step by step on one running example. Every
   calculation (market size, unit cost, contribution margin, break-even, cash runway, equity dilution) is declared in
   `rework/math-checks.md` and recomputed by the build's math gate. Numbers in invented examples are marked as
   illustrative and use Egyptian pounds (EGP).
6. **Corrected models.** The source's definitions and models are corrected, not copied (`design/errata-seed.md`):
   entrepreneurship is defined through opportunity and innovation and covers services; Hofstede has six dimensions
   with his own names and a stated scale direction; informal models (the 4 Ds, the four stages) are labelled informal
   and set beside cited ones; every reused question is re-derived from a cited definition.
7. **One running example.** A small venture founded by two graduates of the faculty, a home-sampling laboratory
   service with a teleradiology partner ("SampleHome", invented and labelled as such), runs through Parts III and IV, so
   each tool is applied to the same idea and the chapters build on each other.

## 3. Structure

Fourteen chapters in four parts. Parts I and II are the three source lectures in their order; Lecture 1 is split into
three chapters and each of Lectures 2 and 3 becomes one chapter (the brief allows merging and splitting). Parts III
and IV are the venture-creation path.

| Part | Chapters | Content |
|---|---|---|
| I — The Entrepreneur in Health Care | ch01–ch03 | What entrepreneurship is, who entrepreneurs are and how they develop, and why a country needs them |
| II — The Environment of a Health Venture | ch04–ch05 | The task and general environment, competition and barriers to entry; culture, globalisation and teams |
| III — From a Health Need to a Venture Design | ch06–ch09 | Need finding, ideas and personas, customer discovery, design thinking and the MVP |
| IV — Building and Sustaining the Venture | ch10–ch14 | Business model, costs and funding, regulation and ethics, IP and brand, launch, growth and social entrepreneurship |

### Chapter shape

1. **Learning Objectives** — 3 to 5, boxed, measurable verbs, each assessed.
2. **Body** — numbered sections of explained prose, mean sentence length at most 16 words.
3. **Required boxes** — at least one each of Egyptian Entrepreneur, Through Two Lenses, In Practice and Ethics Check.
4. **Optional boxes** — Key Formula, Worked Example, Common Mistake, Egyptian Context, as the material needs.
5. **Key Takeaways** — 5 to 8 lines.
6. **Check Your Understanding** — 10 four-option MCQs tagged to objectives (keys balanced, 2–3 per letter, no run
   longer than two), then three short essay questions, at least one of them a health-venture scenario.
7. **Answers and Worked Solutions** — every MCQ with its rationale; every essay question with model answer points.
8. **References** — APA 7, the works the chapter cites; the book closes with the consolidated list.
