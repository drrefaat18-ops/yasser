Drug information Sources :

Tertiary resources :
-provide information that has been summarized and distilled
by the author or editor to provide a quick easy summary of a
topic These include not only textbooks and compendia but also
electronic databases (e.g., Micromedex, Lexicomp) and review
articles.
-The best tertiary resources are written by experts in the field
and are peer reviewed. If the tertiary resource is not current or
comprehensive, a secondary resource should be consulted to
locate primary literature on the topic. However, some
questions can only be answered by using tertiary sources.

Advantages of Tertiary Resources:
These references may often serve as an initial place to identify
information, due to:
1. They provide a fairly complete and concise overview of information
available on a specific topic.
2. These resources are also convenient.
3. easy to use
4.familiar to most practitioners.

Disadvantages of Tertiary Resources:
1. The lag time associated with publication of print copy
tertiary resources, resulting in less current information.
2. The potential for author biases to be introduced
3. The fact that the information may be incomplete,
misleading, or inaccurate .

What is randomization
Randomization is the process of assigning clinical
●
trial participants to treatment groups.
Randomization gives each participant a known
●
and equal chance of being assigned to any of the
groups.
Successful randomization requires that group
●
assignment cannot be predicated in advance.

Advantages of randomization
Elimination of selection Baias.
●
Allows for comparability between
●
groups.
Balances arms with respect to
●
prognostic values.

Disadvantages of randomization
Trials which test for efficacy may not be widely
●
applicable.
Trials which test for effectiveness are larger and
●
more expensive.
Results may not always mimic real life treatment
●
situation (e.g. inclusion / exclusion criteria; highly
controlled setting)

Methods of randomization
Simple method randomization.
●
Unequal allocation randomization.
●
Stratified randomization.
●
Blocked randomization.
●

Unequal allocation randomization.
Unequal allocation randomization is a method used in
●
clinical trials where participants are assigned to study
groups (e.g., intervention and control groups) in proportions
that deviate from the typical 1:1 ratio.
Instead, the allocation may follow a ratio such as 2:1, 3:1, or
●
even more extreme ratios.
This approach can be strategically applied depending on
●
the objectives of the trial.

Unequal allocation randomization.
When two or more treatments under evaluation have a
●
cost difference:
Substantial cost savings can be achieved by adopting an
○
unequal randomization ratio such as 2:1, with only a modest
loss in statistical power.
Another scenario: When one arm saves lives, and the other
○
(such as placebo/medical care only) does not.
Generally, a randomization ratio of 3:1 will lose considerable
○
statistical power.

Stratified Randomization
Stratified randomization is a method used in clinical trials
●
to ensure that key prognostic factors (confounding
variables) are balanced between treatment groups.
By dividing participants into strata based on these factors
●
and then performing randomization within each stratum,
researchers aim to improve the comparability of groups
.
and reduce variability in outcome estimates

Blinding in Clinical Trials
Blinding keeps participants, clinical trial personnel
●
(e.g., healthcare providers), or assessors (those
collecting outcome data) unaware of an assigned
intervention so that they are not influenced by
that knowledge.
Blinding prevents bias at several stages of a trial,
●
although its relevance varies according to
circumstance.

Adverse drug reaction (W.H.O
Definition)
Adverse drug reaction is defined as any response to a drug
●
that is noxious and unintended and that occurs at doses
used in man for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy of
disease or for modification of physiological function.

Side-effects
A consequence of the pharmacological mechanism of action
●
of the drug - usually due to the presence of receptors in a
number of tissues or lack of receptor specificity.
Examples include drowsiness with the older antihistamines,
●
constipation with opioids, etc.
In some situations, a 'side-effect' can be a therapeutic effect,
●
e.g., antihistamines for sedation, opioids for diarrhea

The burden of ADRs
Causality:
●
The probability that a particular medicine is
○
responsible for an isolated effect or ADR.
Adverse drug reactions are frequently serious
●
enough to result in admission to hospital. It is well
recognized that adverse drug reactions place a
significant burden on the health service

Studies have shown adverse drug reactions
●
account for 1 in 16 hospital admissions, and
for 4% of hospital bed capacity.
ADRs and events constitute a serious
●
problem increasing morbidity and mortality
and health care costs worldwide.”

Type A (Augmented) reactions
Reactions which can be predicted from the known
●
pharmacology of the drug. Dose dependent, can be
alleviated by a dose reduction. Examples include:
Anticoagulants: Bleeding,
○
Beta blockers: Bradycardia,
○

Prazosin: Postural hypotension."

Type B (Bizarre) reactions
Cannot be predicted from the pharmacology of the drug.
●
Not dose dependent. Host-dependent factors important in
predisposition. Examples include:
Penicillin: Anaphylaxis,
○
Anticonvulsant: Hypersensitivity.
○

Type C (Chemical) reactions
Biological characteristics can be predicted from
●
the chemical structure of the drug/metabolite.
Example:
Paracetamol: Hepatotoxicity.
○

Type E (End of treatment)
reactions
Occur on withdrawal, especially when the drug is
●

Phenytoin withdrawal: Seizures,
○
Steroid withdrawal: Adrenocortical insufficiency.
○

Type F or Failure (Unexpected
